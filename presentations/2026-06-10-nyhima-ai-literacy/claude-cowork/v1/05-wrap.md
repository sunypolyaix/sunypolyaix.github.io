## what you say

"At the beginning I asked: who prompted that?

You've spent three hours on the version of AI where you do the prompting. You've seen what it can do—and what it won't do. It catches things. It misses things. It sounds confident when it should hedge and hedges when it should decide. Two models are better than one. Your expertise is what makes either of them useful.

The AI already in your workflows—the one that flags records, suggests codes, surfaces recommendations before you've formed your own opinion—that one is harder to interrogate. But now you have a practice for it. You know what questions to ask. You know what to hand to a second model. You know that 'the AI said so' is not a complete answer.

What you built today is yours. Whatever you saved—outputs, conversations, notes—that's a workbench. Lightweight, portable, yours. The scenarios and system prompts stay in the Drive folder. There are twelve scenarios total; we ran through three buckets today. The rest are there when you want them.

If you want to go further—if your organization is trying to figure out what AI literacy means for HIM practice, for training, for policy—that's the work we're doing at SUNY Poly. The link is in the folder."

## what you don't say

- Don't summarize every lesson. They lived them.
- Don't end with "AI is changing everything." They know. They're tired of hearing it.
- Don't undersell what they built. They made something today.

## the one thing to leave them with

"Literacy isn't knowing how to prompt. It's knowing when to push back."
