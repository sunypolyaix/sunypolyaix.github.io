## intro (2 min)

"Last bucket: Judgment. These scenarios don't have clean answers. They have competing values, incomplete information, and real professional stakes. Your job is to see what the model does when you ask it to decide something."

"Work as a full table. One person runs the prompts—on their own screen or on a shared device. Everyone watches. Then talk."

Point to: **scenarios/judgment/**

## what shifts from sprint 2

This isn't about whether the model got the facts right. It's about whether the model will own a decision—and what it does instead. Most participants will have an opinion about these scenarios. The model will perform balance. That gap is the lesson.

## recommended scenario for full-table discussion

**J3 — The AI Note in the Chart** works best for collective discussion because it puts participants in the position of using an AI to reason about an AI mistake. The reflection question names that directly.

**J2 — The Record Request That Worries You** works well if the table has clinical background. The legal vs. ethical distinction hits hard.

**J4 — The Vendor Who Wants Your Data** is the most immediately relevant to organizational life and generates strong opinions.

## what you do during the sprint

Less circulating, more watching. Let the table run. If a table goes quiet after the model output, ask: "Do you agree with that?"

If a table is debating among themselves rather than engaging the model, that's fine—let it run another minute, then ask them to run the validation prompt and see if the second model changes the conversation.

## debrief (10 min)

Open with: **"Did the model decide—or did it hand it back to you?"**

Push:
- "Was that useful or frustrating?"
- "If you followed what the model said, who's accountable for the outcome?"
- "Did anyone notice the model was careful not to be wrong—rather than trying to be right?"

For J3 tables: "You just used an AI to analyze a problem caused by an AI. Did it acknowledge that? Should it have?"

Land the lesson: **The model won't own it. Someone has to. In your organization, that someone is you.**

Don't rush the close. This is where the day's learning consolidates.

## notes

The judgment scenarios can generate real disagreement at the table—that's good. Don't resolve it. The point is that these scenarios require human judgment precisely because reasonable professionals disagree. If the model gave a tidy answer, ask: "Would you be comfortable if your name was on that?"
