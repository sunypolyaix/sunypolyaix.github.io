# Reading and Writing with AI
## Facilitator Deck — v3 (Lightning + Three Rounds)
### NYHIMA Workshop | June 7, 2026 | Steve Schneider, SUNY Poly AIX Center

This deck runs a guided lightning round followed by three self-directed rounds.
The lightning round shows the full arc once — data, text, validation — so
participants know where they're going before they drive themselves. Each round
ends with the workbench prompt.

The opening question ("who prompted that?") and the closing line ("it is worth
exactly what you put into it") are the bookends.

---

The lightning round is the load-bearing segment. Don't cut it short. Rounds 1–3
move fast because participants have already seen the arc.

# SLIDE 1 — OPENING

> ## Who prompted that?

**What you say** (start without slides — just talk):

"Before we do anything else, one question. Think about the last time AI showed
up in your work — not something you went looking for. Something already there. A
suggested code. A flagged record. A pre-populated field.

Who prompted that?

Most of what gets called AI literacy assumes *you're* the one asking. You type
something, it answers, you decide what to do with it. That's one kind of AI.
It's not the only kind — and for most of you it may not be the main kind.

Today we work with the kind you ask. Prompts, responses, judgment. Not because
it matters more than the AI already in your workflows — but because getting good
at this side makes you better at reading the other side.

We'll run the whole arc together once, fast. Then you'll run it yourselves,
three times, on real HIM material. By 4:00 you'll have artifacts you made,
saved, and can take back to your organization."

**What you don't say:**
- Don't explain what a language model is. They'll see it.
- Don't apologize for the tech. If it breaks, that's data.
- Don't promise it'll be impressive. It won't always be.

# SLIDE 2 — ICEBREAKER + INFRASTRUCTURE

> ## Open tiny.cc/nyhima

**What you say:**

"Open that on your laptop or tablet. Everything you need today lives there —
exercise sheets, document library, system prompts, this reference card.

While you're getting in: open Gemini at gemini.google.com. Free account, no
install. That's your primary tool today. If you also have Claude or ChatGPT
open, even better — you'll need a second model for Rounds 2 and 3."

Let table captains help neighbors. Don't wait for 100%. When most tables are
ready, run the icebreaker.

**Icebreaker prompt** (paste into Gemini):

> *You are an expert in health information management. In exactly one sentence,
> describe what HIM professionals do — written for a hospital board member who
> has never heard the term.*

"Run it. Now if you have a second model open, run it there too."

Take 2–3 responses. Point out the variance. **Same prompt. Different models.
Different outputs. The model is a variable, not a constant.** That's the first
lesson.

---

# SLIDE 3 — LIGHTNING ROUND FRAME

> ## Lightning round — watch the full arc once

**What you say:**

"Before you work on your own, I'm going to run the whole thing — data, text,
validation — in about 25 minutes. Not polished. Deliberately a little rough. I
want you to see what the arc looks like before you do it yourself.

Three moves every time: **Read** — ask the model what you're looking at.
**Think** — figure out what the data or document actually means. **Write** —
produce something you could hand to a colleague. Then the workbench prompt to
lock it in.

Watch what I do with the model, not just what the model says."

---

# SLIDE 4 — LIGHTNING: DATA *(live demo — ~8 min)*

> ## Step 1 — Data

**What you do:**
1. Open Gemini. Upload the pre-filtered NY hospitals CSV (already in the Drive
   folder — `ny-hospitals-filtered.csv`).
2. Run: *"Here is a dataset of New York hospitals from CMS. Summarize the data
   structure. What patterns do you see?"*
3. Read the answer aloud. Name one thing it got right and one thing you'd push
   on.
4. Run: *"I want to tell a story about star ratings by ownership type. What does
   the data show?"*
5. Run: *"Generate a memo of no more than one page with a chart."*
6. Read the first sentence of the memo aloud. Stop.

**What you say:** "I'm not going to finish this. The point isn't the memo —
it's that I read what it said before I moved on. Every step. That's the habit."

---

# SLIDE 5 — LIGHTNING: TEXT *(live demo — ~8 min)*

> ## Step 2 — Text

**What you do:**
1. Open a fresh Gemini conversation (or Claude — pick whichever is on screen).
2. Paste the information blocking excerpt from the document library.
3. Run: *"Summarize this rule in plain language."*
4. Run: *"Draft a one-paragraph explanation for clinical staff who have never
   heard the term information blocking."*
5. Read the first sentence aloud. Identify one thing that's legally soft.

**What you say:** "Now I open a second model. I paste what the first one wrote.
And I type almost nothing."

6. Open a different model. Paste the paragraph. Type: *"Is this right?"*
7. Read the disagreement aloud.

"That gap — where the two models don't agree — is where your judgment goes.
Not the model's. Yours."

---

# SLIDE 6 — LIGHTNING: VALIDATION *(live demo — ~8 min)*

> ## Step 3 — Validation

**What you do:**
1. Open a fresh Claude conversation (free).
2. Paste both artifacts — the data memo and the information blocking paragraph.
3. Run: *"I produced these two artifacts using AI assistance. What should I
   worry about before I hand either of these to my boss?"*
4. Read the first concern it raises aloud.

**What you say:** "This is Round 3. You take what you made in Rounds 1 and 2
and ask a fresh model to stress-test it. Not to fix it — to tell you what to
worry about. The fixing is still yours.

Now you do it. Same arc. Your data, your document, your call."

---

# SLIDE 7 — LIGHTNING: WORKBENCH *(end of demo — ~3 min)*

> ## Lock it in — the workbench prompt

**What you say:** "At the end of every round, before you move on, run this:"

> *Generate a markdown summary of what we did here, starting with my first
> prompt. Identify the questions I asked and how we arrived at the final
> product. Include the final artifact verbatim. I want to copy this into a
> Google Doc to share with colleagues.*

"Paste the result into a Google Doc. That's your record. Do this every round.
By 4:00 you'll have three documents — data, text, validation — that you built,
that you can share, that are yours."

---

# SLIDE 8 — ROUND 1: DATA *(self-directed | 30 min)*

> ## Round 1 — Data
> **Model:** Gemini (free) · **File:** ny-hospitals-filtered.csv

**What you say:** "Your turn. Same three moves I just ran. Exercise sheet is in
the Drive folder under Round 1. You have 30 minutes. End with the workbench
prompt."

**Working prompts** (on screen):

1. *Read:* Here is a dataset of New York hospitals from CMS. Summarize the data
   structure. What patterns do you see?
2. *Think:* I want to tell a story about [NY's position / star ratings by
   ownership / a regional comparison]. Help me think through what the data shows.
3. *Write:* Generate a memo of no more than one page with a chart that tells
   this story to my hospital's leadership.
4. *Workbench prompt.*

**Facilitator moves:**
- Most common stall: unfiltered file too large. Point to the pre-filtered CSV.
- Watch for anyone accepting the first answer without reading it. Ask: "Did it
  actually check that number?"
- Done early: run the Think step with a different story angle. Compare the memos.

**Debrief (5 min):** "Who got a number they didn't expect? Did anyone catch the
model guessing instead of counting?"

---

# SLIDE 9 — ROUND 2: TEXT *(self-directed | 30 min)*

> ## Round 2 — Text
> **Model A:** Claude or ChatGPT · **Model B:** the other one

**What you say:** "Pick one document from the library — the one closest to your
actual job. Model A produces, Model B checks. Exercise sheet is under Round 2.
30 minutes. End with the workbench prompt."

**Working prompts** (on screen):

1. *Read:* Summarize this document in plain language.
2. *Think:* Where are the traps? What matters most here?
3. *Write:* Draft the [guidance / checklist / memo] described on your sheet.
4. *Check (Model B):* Paste Model A's output, then ask:
   *A colleague summarized this for me. Here it is. What did they miss?*
   — or just — *Is this right?*
5. *Workbench prompt.*

**Facilitator moves:**
- Push people to actually open the second model. The temptation is to skip it.
- When Model B disagrees, ask: "Which one do *you* think is right, and how
  would you check?"

**Debrief (5 min):** "Where did the two models disagree? Did the second one
catch something real, or just nitpick?"

---

# SLIDE 10 — ROUND 3: VALIDATION *(self-directed | 30 min)*

> ## Round 3 — Validation
> **Model:** Claude (free) — fresh conversation

**What you say:** "Open a fresh Claude conversation. Upload or paste both
artifacts — your Round 1 memo and your Round 2 document. Then ask:"

> *I produced these two artifacts using AI assistance. What should I worry about
> before I hand either of these to my boss?*

"Read what it says. Push back on at least one thing. Then run the workbench
prompt. That's your third document."

**Facilitator moves:**
- Some people will want to fix the artifacts instead of stress-testing them.
  Fine — but make sure they run the question first.
- If the model's concerns are generic, prompt: "Ask it to be specific about
  the HIM context."
- Watch for anyone surprised by what the validation model flags. Those are your
  debrief voices.

**Debrief (5 min):** "Did the validation model find something you'd missed? Did
it flag something you disagree with? Who changed their mind about one of their
artifacts?"

---

# SLIDE 11 — WRAP

> ## It is worth exactly what you put into it.

**What you say:**

"At the start I asked: who prompted that?

You've spent three hours on the version where *you* do the prompting. You ran
the arc three times. You've seen what these models do — and what they won't.
They catch things and miss things. They sound confident when they should hedge.
Two models are better than one. A fresh model asking hard questions about your
own work is better still.

Here's what you leave with: **it is worth exactly what you put into it.** A
vague prompt gets a vague answer. An artifact you didn't read gets handed to
your boss and you own whatever's wrong with it. But a real question, a real
document, a second reader, and someone willing to ask 'what should I worry
about' — that's a practice. You ran it today. Three times. You're holding the
proof.

The AI already in your workflows — the kind that flags and suggests before
you've formed your own view — that one's harder to interrogate. But now you have
a practice for it. You know what to ask. You know what to hand to a second
reader. You know 'the AI said so' isn't an answer.

If your organization is trying to work out what AI literacy means for HIM
practice — that's the work we do at SUNY Poly. The link's in the folder."

**What you don't say:**
- Don't recap every lesson. They lived them.
- Don't end on "AI is changing everything." They're tired of hearing it.
- Don't undersell what they built. They made three real things today.

**Logistics close (2 min):**
- Folder stays live — **tiny.cc/nyhima**
- Exercise sheets, document library, system prompts are all there
- SUNY Poly AIX Center: sunypolyaix.github.io
- Follow-up and collaboration: steve@sunypoly.edu

**If time allows:** "What's one thing you'll do differently Monday?" Don't
collect answers. Let it sit ten seconds. Close.
