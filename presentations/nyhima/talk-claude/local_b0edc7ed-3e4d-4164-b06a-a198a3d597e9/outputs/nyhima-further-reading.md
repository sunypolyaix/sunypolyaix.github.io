# Reading and Writing with AI — Further Reading

*Steve Schneider · AIX Center, SUNY Polytechnic Institute · NYHIMA Annual Conference, June 9, 2026*

A short guide to the sources and ideas behind this talk, organized by its themes. The figures cited from the podium are documented here; the conceptual anchors are listed for those who want to go further.

---

## Why we use it

Brynjolfsson, E., & McAfee, A. (2014). *The Second Machine Age: Work, Progress, and Prosperity in a Time of Brilliant Technologies.* W. W. Norton. — On the distinction between technologies that do existing work faster and those that open genuinely new capacities.

Vallor, S. (2024). *The AI Mirror: How to Reclaim Our Humanity in an Age of Machine Thinking.* Oxford University Press. — On adopting tools deliberately rather than by institutional reflex or fear of being left behind.

---

## The material substrate

International Energy Agency. (2025). *Energy and AI.* IEA, Paris. https://www.iea.org/reports/energy-and-ai — Source for the talk's electricity figures: data-center electricity use ≈ 415 TWh in 2024 (~1.5% of global electricity), projected to roughly double to ≈ 945 TWh by 2030, with AI the fastest-growing driver. *(Contested point: 2030 projections vary by scenario; the ~945 TWh figure is the IEA base case. Verify against the latest IEA update before the talk, as the agency revises these numbers periodically.)*

Li, P., Yang, J., Islam, M. A., & Ren, S. (2025). Making AI Less "Thirsty": Uncovering and Addressing the Secret Water Footprint of AI Models. *Communications of the ACM, 68*(7), 54–63. https://doi.org/10.1145/3724499 — Earlier preprint: arXiv:2304.03271, https://arxiv.org/abs/2304.03271. Source for the water figures: training a large model on the order of ~700,000 liters of fresh water; a short exchange ≈ the volume of a small bottle. *(Contested point: the ~700,000 L estimate is for training GPT-3 in specific Microsoft U.S. data centers and is highly location- and method-dependent; cite it as an order-of-magnitude illustration, not a fixed constant.)*

Epoch AI. (2025). *How much energy does ChatGPT use?* https://epoch.ai/gradient-updates/how-much-energy-does-chatgpt-use — Source for the per-query figure: an ordinary query to a common model ≈ 0.3 Wh (consistent with OpenAI's stated ~0.34 Wh). *(Contested point: per-query energy is genuinely disputed and varies enormously by model and query length — newer, larger "reasoning" models are estimated at several to ~20+ Wh per query. The talk uses the conservative low end and explicitly flags the contest. Do not present a single number as settled.)*

Crawford, K. (2021). *Atlas of AI: Power, Politics, and the Planetary Costs of Artificial Intelligence.* Yale University Press. — The anchor for the material-substrate section: the extraction of lithium (South American salt flats), cobalt (Democratic Republic of the Congo), and rare earths that underlies AI hardware, and the labor and environmental costs hidden behind the interface.

Perrigo, B. (2023, January 18). Exclusive: OpenAI Used Kenyan Workers on Less Than $2 Per Hour to Make ChatGPT Less Toxic. *TIME.* https://time.com/6247678/openai-chatgpt-kenya-workers/ — Source for the data-labeling and content-moderation labor account: workers in Kenya, contracted via Sama, paid roughly $1.32–$2/hour to label graphic and traumatic text.

Cottom, T. M., & Gray, M. L. (2019). *Ghost Work: How to Stop Silicon Valley from Building a New Global Underclass.* Houghton Mifflin Harcourt. — On the hidden human labor that makes automated systems appear seamless.

Epoch AI. (2024). *How much does it cost to train frontier AI models?* https://epoch.ai/blog/how-much-does-it-cost-to-train-frontier-ai-models — Source for the training-cost figures: leading models now cost well over $100 million to train, with costs growing roughly 2–3× per year. *(GPT-4 compute-cost estimates range from ~$40M amortized to >$100M all-in depending on methodology; the talk uses the conservative ">$100 million" framing for the most capable current models.)*

## Cognition and the human

Carr, N. (2010). *The Shallows: What the Internet Is Doing to Our Brains.* W. W. Norton. — On how the tools we think with reshape the way we think.

Bainbridge, L. (1983). Ironies of Automation. *Automatica, 19*(6), 775–779. https://doi.org/10.1016/0005-1098(83)90046-8 — The foundational account of how automating a task degrades the human operator's skill at it — the basis for the talk's "deskilling" discussion (delivered in plain language, without clinical vocabulary).

Sparrow, B., Liu, J., & Wegner, D. M. (2011). Google Effects on Memory: Cognitive Consequences of Having Information at Our Fingertips. *Science, 333*(6043), 776–778. https://doi.org/10.1126/science.1207745 — On cognitive offloading: we remember less when we expect a system to hold information for us.

Ong, W. J. (1982). *Orality and Literacy: The Technologizing of the Word.* Methuen. — On the arrival of writing as a transformation of human consciousness — the basis for the talk's comparison between this shift and the invention of writing.

## Thinking

Plato. *Phaedrus*, 274c–275b (the myth of Theuth and Thamus). Trans. in Cooper, J. M. (Ed.). (1997). *Plato: Complete Works.* Hackett. — The oldest version of the talk's central worry: that a technology which stores and produces our thinking for us can hollow out the very capacity it appears to extend. Writing, Thamus warns, will produce "the appearance of wisdom, not true wisdom." The anchor for the close.

Arendt, H. (1971). *Thinking* (Volume One of *The Life of the Mind*). Harcourt Brace Jovanovich. — On thinking as an activity that cannot be delegated and remain one's own.

Weizenbaum, J. (1976). *Computer Power and Human Reason: From Judgment to Calculation.* W. H. Freeman. — From the creator of one of the first chatbots: on the difference between what computers can do and what we should ask them to do, and on judgment as a distinctly human act.

---

*A note on the numbers: the substrate figures above are accurate to the best sources available as of June 2026, but energy, water, and cost estimates in this field move quickly and are actively debated. Where a figure is contested, the contest is noted here rather than stated from the podium. Treat each number as a defensible order of magnitude, not a fixed constant.*
