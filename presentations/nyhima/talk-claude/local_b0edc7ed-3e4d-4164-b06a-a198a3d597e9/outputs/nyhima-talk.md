# Reading and Writing with AI: Why Universal AI Literacy Matters for Health Information Professionals

## 1 · Intro

*~5 minutes · target ~600 words*

Thank you. I want to start by being honest about where we are.

We are in the middle of something. Not at the beginning, where you can see the whole shape of a thing before it arrives. Not at the end, where you can look back and say what it was. We are in the middle. And the truth about the middle of a large change is that the people living through it almost never understand it while it is happening. That understanding comes later. It comes to people who are not yet in this room, who will look back at this decade the way we look back at the arrival of the printing press, or the telephone, or the first computers, and say: that was the turn. They were standing right in it. I doubt they knew.

So I am not going to stand up here and tell you I know what this is. I don't. Nobody does, not really, not yet. What I can do is be honest with you about what we can see from inside the middle of it.

Here is what we can see. There is now a kind of machine that produces language. You type something to it, and it writes back. It drafts the email. It summarizes the report. It answers the question. It appears to reason. It was built from the written record of human knowledge — the books, the articles, the documentation, the billions of pages of things people have written down — and out of all of that it produces sentences that sound like a person wrote them. Often a competent person. Sometimes an expert.

I am not going to explain how it works. I am not going to talk about the engineering, because the engineering is not what matters to you, and because honestly the engineering is not what matters about it at all. What matters is what it does, and what it asks of us, and what it costs. That is what we are here to look at.

So here is where we are going for the next hour.

First I want to ask why. Why do we use this. Not the easy answer — we will get past the easy answer quickly. The real one.

Then I want to ask what it actually is. Not as an idea. As a physical thing in the world, that comes from somewhere, that was made by someone, that runs on something. Because you cannot judge a thing honestly until you know what it costs to make.

Then I want to ask what it does to us. To us as people who think, and read, and write for a living. Because this tool reaches into exactly those things.

And then, at the end, I am going to ask you to think. That is the whole close. Not a list of things to do on Monday. Not a strategy. Just thinking. I will explain why when we get there.

That is the hour. Why we use it, what it is, what it costs, what it does to us, and then thinking.

I am going to go slowly. Some of this is uncomfortable, and I am not going to rush you past the uncomfortable parts, because the discomfort is part of the point. I am not here to sell you anything. I am not here to scare you. I am here to look at this honestly, with you, as adults — which is something almost no one has bothered to do with you about this technology, even as it has been dropped into the middle of your work.

So let's look.

## 2 · Why AI

*~10 minutes · target ~1,200 words*

Why do we use this?

The answer you will hear everywhere is efficiency. It is faster. It saves time. You will hear that in every vendor pitch, every webinar, every memo from administration. And it is true, as far as it goes. But it is the shallow answer, and I want to get past it in the first two minutes, because if efficiency were the whole story this would be a small thing. A better dictation tool. A faster way to do the paperwork. We would not be having this conversation.

The real reason is bigger, and it deserves to be said plainly, without embarrassment.

This technology lets us do things that were not possible before. Not the same work, faster. Different work entirely.

Let me say what I mean. There is a difference between a tool that does your task more quickly and a tool that does a task you simply could not have done. A faster horse gets you to the next town sooner. A telephone lets you speak to someone a thousand miles away in real time — which no amount of faster horse will ever give you. That is a difference in kind. And some of what this technology offers is a difference in kind.

A person who has never been able to write a clean paragraph can now produce one. A clinician staring at a wall of unstructured notes can ask a question of all of it at once and get an answer in seconds. Someone working in a language that is not their first can write in it with confidence. A small office with no analyst can suddenly do analysis. These are not small conveniences. For the people they reach, they are genuinely new capacities. Things that were closed are now open.

I want to be honest that this is real, because a lot of the conversation about AI pretends it isn't. It is real. That is why this is hard. If it were useless we would ignore it and go home. We use it because, some of the time, it does something we could not do, and it does it well enough to matter.

That is the honest answer to why. Now I have to give you the other half of the honest answer, which is the part nobody says out loud.

A great deal of why we use this has nothing to do with whether it helps.

We use it because we are told to. Institutions adopt it because other institutions are adopting it. There is a directive, a mandate, a line in a strategic plan, and underneath the directive there is very often no reasoning at all — no one who can tell you what problem it is solving, or whether it is solving it, or what it costs to run. The decision was made because a decision had to be seen to be made. That is not a reason. It is the appearance of a reason.

We use it out of fear. The fear of being left behind. The fear that the organization down the road has it and we don't, that the younger people coming up know it and we don't, that to question it is to mark yourself as the person who didn't get it. That fear is powerful, and it is almost never examined, because examining it feels like admitting weakness. But a decision made out of fear of being left behind is not a decision about whether the thing is good. It is a decision about how you want to be seen.

And we use it because it is there. This is maybe the quietest reason and the strongest one. The assumption that because a thing exists, and is available, and everyone is talking about it, it must therefore be used. That availability is the same as obligation. It is not. The fact that you can do something has never been a reason to do it. We know this in every other part of life. We seem to forget it completely when a new technology arrives.

I want to dwell on that one, because it is doing more work than it looks like. When a tool is everywhere — on your phone, in your software, in the meeting where someone says we really should be using this — the pressure to adopt it stops feeling like a choice and starts feeling like weather. Something happening to you that you simply respond to. And a tool you adopt because it is in the air is a tool you have never actually decided to use. You have only failed to resist it. Those are different things, and the difference matters, because you cannot use well a thing you never chose. You just drift with it, and you let it set the terms, and one day you look up and your work has been rearranged around a tool you never once sat down and decided was worth it.

So when I ask why we use this, the honest answer is a mix. Some of it is the real thing — the genuinely new capacity, the work that was not possible before. And a lot of it is institutional pressure with nothing behind it, and fear, and the simple gravitational pull of a thing that is suddenly everywhere.

Here is why I am pulling these apart so carefully. If you do not separate the good reasons from the empty ones, you cannot make a real decision about any of it. You just get carried. And the people most likely to get carried are not the careless ones. They are the conscientious ones — the people who want to do right by their work and their patients and their institutions, and who are told that doing right now means adopting this, quickly, without too many questions. The conscientious are the easiest to move, because they do not want to be the obstacle.

I am asking you not to be carried. Not to refuse — I am not telling you to refuse anything. I am asking you to know which reason you are acting on. To be able to say, this part I am using because it genuinely lets me do something I could not do, and this part I am using because I was told to and I have never asked why.

But you cannot do even that much — you cannot weigh the benefit honestly — until you know two more things. You have to know what the thing actually is. And you have to know what it costs. Not what it costs you in dollars, in a subscription. What it costs to exist at all.

That is where most of us have never looked. The tool arrives on the screen, clean and weightless, and it seems to come from nowhere and cost nothing. It does not come from nowhere. It is one of the most physically expensive things human beings have ever built.

So before we go any further into what it does to us, I want to take you to where it actually comes from.

## 3 · What AI is — the material substrate

*~10 minutes · target ~1,200 words*

I want to describe this thing to you as a physical object in the world. Not as an idea. As something made of matter, that sits somewhere, that draws power, that had to be dug out of the ground and assembled by people. Because the screen hides all of that. The screen is the most misleading part of the whole experience. It is clean, and quiet, and instant, and it makes the thing behind it feel like it has no body at all.

It has an enormous body.

Start with where it lives. The model you talk to runs in a data center. A building, or a campus of buildings, full of computers — not the kind on your desk, specialized machines, racks and racks of them, running day and night. These buildings are large, and there are more of them every month, and they are being built faster now than at almost any point in the history of construction, because the demand for this kind of computing is climbing steeply.

Those buildings draw electricity. A lot of it. The best current accounting, from the International Energy Agency, puts the electricity used by data centers worldwide at roughly 415 terawatt-hours in 2024. That is about one and a half percent of all the electricity used on Earth. And it is rising fast — the same agency projects it will roughly double by 2030, to around 945 terawatt-hours. That higher number is more electricity than the entire country of Japan uses in a year. For data centers. And the part of that growth driven specifically by AI is the fastest-growing part of all.

When you send a single query — one question, one request — it draws a small amount of power. The figures here are genuinely contested, and I will be careful. For an ordinary request to a common model, the estimate is somewhere around a third of a watt-hour. That is small. About what an efficient light bulb uses in a couple of minutes. It sounds like nothing. But multiply one small thing by a billion times a day, every day, and the small thing becomes a power plant. That is the shape of this. Each use is nearly weightless. The sum is enormous.

And the newer, more capable models — the ones that appear to reason, that work through a problem before they answer — draw considerably more than that per query. Some estimates put them many times higher. The trend is not toward each query getting cheaper. As the models get more capable, each use tends to cost more, not less, and we are using them more, not less. The two curves point the same direction.

Then there is water. The computers generate heat, and the heat has to be carried away, and a great deal of that cooling is done with water. Fresh water — often drinking-quality water, because that is what runs through the pipes. Researchers who have studied this estimate that training one large model a few years ago consumed on the order of seven hundred thousand liters of fresh water. And a short back-and-forth with a chatbot — a handful of exchanges — uses roughly the amount of water in a small bottle. Again: tiny, per use. Enormous, summed. And some of these data centers are being built in places that are already short of water, where that draw competes with farms and households.

Now go further back, before the building, before the power. Go to the hardware itself. The specialized chips that make this possible are made of materials that come out of the ground. Rare earth elements. Lithium. Cobalt. These are mined — lithium from the salt flats of South America, where the extraction draws down water on land that indigenous communities depend on; cobalt from mines in central Africa, under labor conditions that, when you read about them honestly, are difficult to read about. The clean chip in the clean machine begins in a pit, with someone digging. That is not a metaphor. That is the supply chain.

And then there are the people. This is the part that gets erased most completely. The model did not teach itself to be helpful and safe on its own. People taught it. Tens of thousands of people, doing work that has a name now — data labeling, content moderation — sitting at screens, sorting and tagging and correcting, telling the machine this is good, this is not, this is acceptable, this is not. Much of that work is done in the Global South, for very low pay. In one well-documented case, workers in Kenya were paid somewhere between about a dollar thirty and two dollars an hour to read and label the worst material on the internet — descriptions of abuse, of violence, of things I will not describe in this room — so that the rest of us would never have to see it when we use the tool. So that the experience would feel clean. Some of those workers carry what they read with them now. The clean experience was built on someone else absorbing the unclean part.

So let me put the whole object in front of you. A building drawing the power of a small nation. Fresh water turned to vapor to keep it cool. Minerals torn out of contested ground. A hidden workforce, poorly paid, doing the work that makes the surface feel effortless. And on top of all of it, the model — which was extraordinarily expensive to build in the first place. The leading models now cost well over a hundred million dollars each just to train, and that cost has been climbing several times over every couple of years. And once it is built, it is never free to run. Every single query draws power from a grid somewhere. There is no such thing as a free question.

I am not telling you these numbers to make you feel guilty for using the tool. Guilt is not useful and it is not the point. I am telling you because almost no one using this technology has ever been shown the bill. The whole design of the thing is to hide the bill. It arrives on your screen weightless and instant and free-seeming, and behind that screen is one of the heaviest, thirstiest, most extractive industrial operations human beings have ever assembled.

You cannot judge whether something is worth its cost if you have been carefully prevented from ever seeing the cost. So now you have seen it. The convenience is real. The bill is also real. Hold both.

And there is one more cost — the one that is hardest to see, because it is not in a data center or a mine or a grid. It is in us. That is the next thing I want to look at.

## 4 · What AI does to us as humans

*~15 minutes · target ~1,800 words · deliver ~1,600 — go slowly, leave space*

I want to make a large claim now, and then I want to spend the rest of our time being careful about it.

The claim is this. Of all the technologies human beings have invented, this one may be as significant as the invention of writing itself.

That sounds like an overstatement. Let me show you why I don't think it is.

Almost every tool we have ever made does work in the world. A plow turns soil. A loom weaves cloth. An engine moves a load. Even our information machines, until now, mostly moved and stored — the telephone carried your words, the computer filed them, the search engine fetched them. They handled thought from the outside. They moved it around. They kept it. They did not produce it.

This is different. This technology operates in the medium of thought itself. It produces language. It produces what looks like reasoning. It writes. And reading, reasoning, and writing are not side activities for people like you. They are the work. They are what a health information professional does all day — read, judge, interpret, document, decide, write it down. This tool reaches directly into that. Not into the filing. Into the thinking and the writing that the filing was always just a record of.

That is why I reach for the comparison to writing. When writing was invented, it did not just give people a new tool. It changed what a human mind was for. Before writing, you held your knowledge in memory — you were your knowledge, you carried it. After writing, you could put it outside yourself, onto a surface, and the surface would hold it for you. That was an extraordinary gain. We would have no science, no law, no records, no medicine without it. But it was also a loss, and the people alive at the time felt the loss clearly. Something that had lived inside human beings now lived outside them. The memory that no longer had to hold everything began, slowly, to hold less.

There is a worry as old as writing itself — that a technology which stores our thinking for us can quietly hollow out the very thing it appears to strengthen. And here is the distinction I want you to carry, quietly, for the rest of this talk. Writing only stored thought. It held it still so you could come back to it. This technology does something writing never did. It produces the thought. It does the thinking part, or appears to, and hands you the result.

So what does that do to us?

Let me start with the most ordinary version, the one you can watch happen in yourself.

When a machine offers to do a piece of thinking for you, and you let it, the thinking does not happen in you. The first time, that feels like a gift. You were going to have to draft the difficult paragraph, and now it is drafted. You were going to have to work through the problem, and now there is an answer on the screen. The effort you would have spent, you didn't. That is the appeal, and it is genuine.

But here is what we know about human capacity, and we have known it for a long time, from every domain where machines took over part of the work. A capacity that goes unused does not stay where you left it. It fades. The skill you do not practice gets weaker. This is not a moral failing and it is not laziness. It is simply how people work. The muscle you stop using atrophies. The route you always navigate by the map, you never learn. The arithmetic you always hand to the calculator, you slowly lose the feel for. Use built the skill in the first place, and the absence of use lets it go.

Now apply that to thinking and to writing — to judgment, to the work you do. If, every time there is a hard paragraph to write, the machine writes it, then over time the capacity to write the hard paragraph weakens in you. If, every time there is a judgment to make, you reach for the machine's judgment first, then the muscle of making the judgment yourself gets less practice, and less, and the day comes when you are not sure you could make it alone. The tool that was supposed to support your expertise can, used a certain way, quietly stand in for it until the expertise behind it has thinned out.

And there is a sharper version of this, and I want to name it carefully because it is the one that worries me most. There is a difference between losing a skill you once had and never building it at all.

Think about the people coming up behind you. The new professional, early in a career, for whom the machine was there from the first day. For that person, the machine does not replace a skill they built and then let fade. It intercepts the work before they ever build the skill in the first place. The hard years of doing the work yourself — the years that are frustrating and slow and that feel like wasted effort while you are in them — those are the years that build judgment. They are supposed to be hard. The difficulty is not a flaw in the process. The difficulty is the process. If a machine does the hard part before the person ever struggles through it, the struggle never happens, and the judgment the struggle would have built never forms. You can end up with a generation of professionals who can operate the tool beautifully and who never developed the underlying capacity the tool was imitating. That is a different and deeper thing than deskilling. That is a skill that simply never arrives.

There is one more piece to this, and it is about trust, and it is subtle. When a machine gives you an answer, and it gives it confidently, in clean fluent language, something in us tends to accept it. The fluency reads as competence. The confidence reads as correctness. And so we stop checking. We stop forming our own independent view first and then comparing. We read the answer, it sounds right, it sounds sure, and we go with it. The trouble is that this kind of machine can be completely wrong while sounding exactly as confident as when it is right. It has no signal that tells you which is which. The fluency is identical. And the more we lean on it, the less we maintain the independent footing we would need to catch it when it is wrong. We hand over the work. And then, without noticing, we hand over the judgment about whether the work is any good — which was the part that actually required us.

Now I want to widen out from the individual to the room, because this is not only personal. It is social, and some of it is happening to this profession, right now, in this building.

Work is being restructured around this technology. Tasks that used to take a trained person are being reassigned to a tool plus a less-trained person, or to a tool plus no one. And here is the quiet part. When a machine can approximate the output of hard-won professional judgment — can produce something that looks close enough — then the people deciding budgets begin to ask what that judgment is still worth. Not whether the machine is actually as good. Whether it is good enough that the difference can be allowed to disappear from the spreadsheet. That question is being asked about a lot of careful, skilled, human work right now. Some of it is the work done by people in this room. I am not going to stand here and pretend otherwise. You would not believe me if I did.

I want to be careful here, because there is a hysterical version of everything I just said, and I am not interested in the hysterical version. I am not telling you the machines are coming for all of you tomorrow. I am not telling you to be afraid. Fear makes people stupid, and you cannot think clearly while you are panicking, and thinking clearly is the entire point of this hour.

What I am telling you is the honest, undramatic version. Capacities fade when they go unused. Skills that are intercepted early never form. Confident output earns more trust than it has earned the right to. And work, and the value placed on human expertise, is being quietly reshaped while most of the people affected have not been given a clear enough picture to even see it happening.

I am diagnosing. I am not prescribing. I do not have a six-point plan to hand you, and I would not trust one if someone tried to hand it to me. What I have is a clear-eyed account of what this technology does to the people who use it, and a belief that you cannot respond well to something you have not first seen clearly.

So we have looked at why we use it. We have looked at what it physically is and what it costs the world. And now we have looked at what it does to us — to our skill, our judgment, our trust, our work.

There is one human capacity I have circled the whole time and not yet named directly. It is the one underneath all the others. It is the one thing in all of this that cannot be handed off to a machine without something essential being lost. I want to spend our last fifteen minutes there.

## 5 · Thinking — the close

*~15 minutes · target ~1,800 words · deliver ~1,600 — slowest section, let it land*

I want to talk about thinking.

Not as a skill on a list. Not as something to optimize. Thinking — the plain act of working something out in your own mind. Holding a question. Turning it over. Being confused, and staying with the confusion, and slowly coming to understand. That.

Here is the thing about thinking that I keep coming back to. It is the one act that cannot be handed off without ceasing to be yours.

You can hand off the writing. The machine will produce sentences. You can hand off the search, the summary, the first draft, the calculation. All of that can be done for you, and the result can be real, and you can use it. But the moment you hand off the thinking — the actual working-out, the part where you hold the thing in your own mind and come to understand it — the moment you hand that off, it is no longer your thought. There is an answer on the screen, but you did not think it. And an answer you did not think is not knowledge that you have. It is just a thing in your possession that you are trusting. You own it the way you own a fact you read on a sign. It is not in you. You did not earn it. And when the sign is gone, you have nothing.

This is the difference between having an answer and understanding something. The machine is very good at giving you answers. It cannot give you understanding, because understanding is not a thing that can be transferred. It is not a file. It only ever forms one way: a mind does the work. There is no version of understanding that you receive without thinking. The thinking is not the means to the understanding. The thinking is the understanding. They are the same event.

And reading is part of this, more than we usually admit. We talk about reading as if it were just intake — getting the information off the page and into your head. But real reading is not intake. It is thinking, done with someone else's words as the occasion for it. When you read something hard and slow, and you stop, and you go back, and you argue with it in your head, you are not absorbing it. You are thinking it through. That is why a summary is never the same as having read the thing. The machine will summarize anything for you now — a report, an article, a whole book — and the summary will be accurate, and you will have the gist, and you will not have done the reading. You will have the conclusions without the journey that made the conclusions mean anything. And a profession that stops reading for itself, that takes the gist and skips the thinking that reading is, slowly loses the ability to tell a good argument from a bad one. Because that ability was built by reading. It does not come from the summary.

So when I say the thinking is what is at stake, I mean the reading too, and the writing too, because those are not separate from thinking. They are thinking, done on a page. When you write something out yourself — really write it, struggle with the sentence until it says what you actually mean — you usually discover that you did not fully know what you meant until you wrote it. The writing was not the recording of a finished thought. The writing was where the thought got finished. Hand that off, and you get clean sentences and an unfinished mind. The page looks done. The thinking never happened.

And that is why, when people ask me what they should do about all of this — and they always ask, they want the close to be a list, they want three things to do on Monday morning — I don't give them the list.

I have spent this hour being honest with you. I told you why we use this, including the reasons that don't hold up. I showed you what it physically costs — the power, the water, the minerals, the hidden workers. I described what it does to us — the skills that fade, the judgment that thins, the trust we give too easily, the work being quietly reshaped underneath us. That was forty minutes of honest, sometimes uncomfortable accounting.

And the payoff to all of that is not a checklist. It would be a betrayal of everything I just said to end with a checklist. Because a checklist is one more thing handed to you, one more piece of thinking done somewhere else and passed to you finished, one more answer you did not work out yourself. If I gave you five rules for using AI responsibly, you would write them down, and you would feel better, and you would have done no thinking at all. I would have done exactly what I am warning you about. I would have handed you the conclusion and skipped the part that was yours.

So I am not going to do that. The payoff is the thinking itself. It is yours to do, and it is the one part of all of this that no one — not me, not a vendor, not your administration, not the machine — can do for you without it stopping being yours.

What I can do is point you at the questions. Not answer them. Point at them.

When you go back to your work, and the tool is there, and it offers to do a piece of your thinking for you, I want you to be able to notice the offer as it happens. Just notice it. To feel the small moment where you could do the thinking yourself or let it be done, and to know that the moment is a real choice and not a foregone one. That noticing is itself a kind of thinking, and it is most of the battle. Most of what gets handed off gets handed off without anyone deciding anything. It just slides. To make it a decision again — even sometimes — is to take it back.

I want you to be able to ask, honestly, of any given task: is this a piece of work I want done, or is this a piece of thinking I want to have done? Because those are different, and the tool does not distinguish between them, and it will take the second as readily as the first. There is work you should be glad to hand off. And there is thinking that, if you hand it off, you will have quietly given away a piece of what makes you good at what you do. Telling those apart is not something I can do for you. You are the only one standing close enough to your own work to tell.

And I want you to hold on to the slow, hard, unglamorous parts of your own expertise — the parts the machine offers to spare you. Because those parts, the difficult ones, the ones that take years, are not obstacles between you and your real work. They are your real work. They are where the judgment lives. A profession that lets a machine do all of its hard parts will wake up one day to find it has no hard parts left, and a profession with no hard parts is a profession that anyone, or anything, can do.

I started by saying we are in the middle of something we do not yet understand. That is still true. I cannot tell you how this ends. No one can. But I can tell you that the people who come through a change like this with themselves intact are not the ones who refused the new thing, and they are not the ones who surrendered to it completely. They are the ones who kept thinking the whole way through. Who used the tool without letting it use them. Who knew, at each step, what they were handing over and what they were keeping.

Reading. Thinking. Writing. These are the oldest human acts, and they are exactly the acts this technology reaches into, and they are what is actually at stake here — under all the talk of efficiency and disruption and productivity. The question this technology puts to you, finally, is not a technical one. It is not even really a question about the technology. It is a question about you. How much of your own reading are you willing to keep doing yourself. How much of your own writing. And how much of your own thinking.

I am not going to answer that for you. That would be the one thing I have spent this whole hour asking you not to let anyone do.

I am just going to leave it with you.

Thank you.
