# Context — NYHIMA Talk

## What this is

This is the context behind the task. The task brief (`nyhima-talk-cowork-task.md`)
tells you *what* to write and *how*. This tells you the world it lives in and *why*
it's shaped the way it is. Read both before you start. Where the two seem to conflict,
the task brief governs the specifics; this governs the spirit.

## Whose talk this is

The speaker is Steve Schneider — Professor of Communications and Humanities and
Co-Director of the AIX Center at SUNY Polytechnic Institute, a SUNY AI Fellow for the
Public Good. His work is building an AI literacy framework across the SUNY system. The
talk is his argument, in his voice. Write it as something he would say standing in
front of a room — not as an essay about the topic.

## The frame behind the talk

Steve's working framework treats AI literacy as the ability to read, think, and write
using the tools, techniques, and systems a person's professional peers use — and
treats AI as cognitive infrastructure, not a gadget to be judged from the outside. The
frame is descriptive: it tracks what literate practitioners actually do rather than
prescribing what they should.

This frame is the lens for the whole talk. It is not a term in the talk. Do not name
it, do not introduce an acronym, do not teach it as a model. Its presence should be
felt only in the close, where the talk circles back to reading, thinking, and writing
as the human acts that are actually at stake — without ever labeling them as a
framework.

That frame is also what earns the title. The session is printed as *Reading and
Writing with AI: Why Universal AI Literacy Matters for Health Information
Professionals.* The talk ranges wider than the title suggests — why we use this, what
it physically is, what it does to us, and the act of thinking. But reading, thinking,
and writing are the through-line, and *universal* is the argument: this is not
specialist knowledge for the technical few. Everyone in the room has a stake in
understanding it. The talk should sit credibly under its title without restating it.

## The substance behind Section 4 (stays unnamed)

Section 4 — what AI does to us — has an intellectual backbone Steve works from: the
way automation changes human performance. Skills that go unused atrophy. Attention
degrades when a system is doing the watching. People over-trust confident automated
output and stop forming an independent view. And there is a difference between losing
a skill you once had and never developing it at all — because a machine intercepted
the work before you ever learned to do it yourself.

Deliver these as plain observation. An ordinary word like "deskilling" is fine if it
reads naturally. Do not import the clinical vocabulary — no "automation bias,"
"anchoring," "fixation," "vigilance decrement," "plan continuation bias." That
nomenclature creates distance for this audience. The phenomena are the point; the
jargon is not.

The same discipline applies to the scale claim. The honest comparison Steve draws is
to the invention of writing itself — and behind it sits a worry as old as Plato: that
a technology which stores and produces our thinking for us can hollow out the very
capacity it appears to extend. There is a real distinction worth carrying quietly here
— writing only *stored* thought; this technology *produces* it. Deliver the worry.
Don't cite the source from the podium; it goes in the handout.

## How the talk got to this shape (today's decisions)

The talk went through several frames before landing here. Earlier versions were built
around a distinction between AI you prompt and AI that prompts you, with a
credential-leveled literacy argument and a domain-by-domain matrix. That is cut — too
operational, too structured for this audience.

What replaced it is an honest accounting in five moves: why we use this, what it
actually is, what it costs, what it does to us, and an invitation to think. The
decisions that define it:

- **The audience is basic.** Many do little or nothing with AI directly. Treat them as
  adults; assume no technical fluency.
- **No manufactured edge.** The discomfort emerges from the honesty of the material.
  Do not state a provocative thesis up front — by the end it will be there on its own.
- **No call to action.** The close is not agency, not homework, not a checklist. It is
  thinking. The payoff is the thinking itself.
- **Scholarship lives in a separate handout**, never in the spoken text.
- **Costs are split by kind.** The environmental cost is a physical fact about the
  substrate, so it lives in Section 3. The cognitive and social costs are about people,
  so they live in Section 4.

## Voice and standard

First person — "I." The room is "you." "We" only for genuine all-of-humanity claims.
The prose is stripped: short sentences, concrete nouns, declarative voice. No coaching,
no motivational framing, no hedged academic throat-clearing, no simulated intimacy, no
"that's not X, it's Y." No bullet lists inside the spoken text. The full constraint
list is in the task brief; honor all of it.

## Sources that anchor the argument

Kate Crawford's *Atlas of AI* anchors the material substrate — the data centers,
minerals, energy, and labor behind the systems. Plato's *Phaedrus* (274c–275b) anchors
the scale claim and the worry about thinking. Walter Ong's work on orality and literacy
informs the comparison between this shift and the arrival of writing. The
material-substrate figures must be researched fresh and sourced (see the task brief's
Research section). All of this is apparatus for the handout. None of it is named aloud.
