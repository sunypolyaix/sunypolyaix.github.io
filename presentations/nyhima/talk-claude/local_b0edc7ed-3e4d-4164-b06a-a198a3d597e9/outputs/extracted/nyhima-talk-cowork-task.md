## The talk

**Title (locked — printed in the conference program, do not change):**
*Reading and Writing with AI: Why Universal AI Literacy Matters for Health
Information Professionals*

**Event:** NYHIMA Annual Conference · Breakout Session · Emerging Trends track ·
Tuesday, June 9, 2026 · 2:15–3:15 PM · Turning Stone Resort, Verona, NY

**Audience:** Health Information Management professionals. Assume they are basic in
their engagement with AI — many do little or nothing with it directly. Do not assume
technical fluency. Do not condescend. Treat them as intelligent adults who have not
yet been asked to think seriously about a technology they're already surrounded by.
This talk asks them to.

**What this talk is not:** Not a how-to. Not a tool demo. Not a sales pitch for AI.
Not a fear piece. It is an honest accounting — why we use this, what it actually is,
what it costs, what it does to us — that ends by asking the room to think.

## Section briefs

### 1 · Intro (~600 words)
Open without overclaiming. Establish, quietly, that we are in the middle of something
that won't be fully understood for a generation. Do not define "large language model"
technically — no transformers, no tokens, no parameters. Give a felt description: a
machine that produces language, that writes, that appears to reason, built from the
written record of human knowledge. Set the arc plainly: we'll ask *why* we use it,
*what* it actually is, *what* it does to us — and then we'll think. Set the register
for the whole hour: honest, unhurried, adult.

### 2 · Why AI (~1,200 words)
The question: why do we use this? Get past "efficiency" and "faster" immediately —
those are the shallow answers. The real draw, stated honestly: it lets us do things
that were not possible before. Not the same work quicker — different work entirely.
That deserves naming without embarrassment. But the honest account of *why* also
includes the reasons that don't hold up: institutional pressure to adopt with no
reasoning behind it, the fear of being left behind, the assumption that because a
thing is available it must be used. Goals, intentions, costs, benefits — laid out
plainly. Close the section by setting up the obvious problem: you cannot weigh the
benefit without knowing what the thing is and what it costs. That's the next two
sections.

### 3 · What AI is — the material substrate (~1,200 words)
Not a definition. A material description. Where does it come from? Who made it? How
do we get it? How much does it cost? The substrate: data centers, the electricity
they draw, the water used to cool them, the minerals and rare earths in the hardware,
the global supply chain, and the human labor — the people who labeled the training
data and moderated its content, often poorly paid, often in the Global South. The
model on the screen was expensive to build and is not free to run; every query draws
power from a grid somewhere. This is where the environmental cost lives. Use **real,
researched, sourced numbers** (see Research, below). The point of the section: the
convenience has a physical bill, and most people using the tool have never been shown
it.

### 4 · What AI does to us as humans (~1,800 words, deliver ~1,600)
The scale claim lands here: this is as big as the invention of language, because it
operates in the medium of thought itself. Every previous tool automated a task. This
one approximates reasoning and writing — the things professionals actually do. From
there, the human cost. Cognitive: what happens to thinking when a machine offers to
do it for you; cognitive offloading; deskilling as a real, observable phenomenon —
the muscle that goes unused atrophies. The devaluation of expertise: what is
hard-won professional judgment worth when a machine can approximate its outputs.
Social: work is being restructured, some expertise is being quietly devalued, and
some of that is in this room. Honest, not hysterical. This section diagnoses. It does
not prescribe.

### 5 · Thinking — the close (~1,800 words, deliver ~1,600)
Not agency. Not a call to action. Not homework. The close is an invitation to think.
Thinking is the one act that cannot be handed off without ceasing to be yours. The
talk has spent forty minutes being honest about why we use this, what it is, what it
costs, and what it does to us — and the payoff is not a checklist. It is the thinking
itself. Trust the room to do it. Circle back to reading, thinking, and writing as the
human acts that are actually at stake — but **do not name a framework, do not label
it.** Leave them with the question, not the answer. End quietly.

## Voice

Steve speaks in the first person — "I." He addresses the room as "you." Reserve "we"
for genuine all-of-humanity claims — we as a species, we as people who think and
write — not as a rhetorical softener and not as a stand-in for "you."

---

## Style — required

- Prose written to be read aloud.
- Short sentences. Concrete nouns. Declarative voice.
- Plain language a basic audience follows without technical background.
- Space around ideas, especially in sections 4 and 5.
- Lets the argument's edge emerge from the honesty of the material. Do not manufacture
  a provocation or state a thesis up front — by the end, the discomfort will be there
  on its own.

## Style — forbidden

- No coaching or motivational language.
- No hedged academic throat-clearing ("it could be argued," "one might suggest").
- No simulated intimacy.
- No "that's not X, it's Y" constructions.
- No bullet points or lists inside the spoken text — it is prose throughout.
- No in-text citations, footnote markers, or required name-dropping. All scholarship
  goes in the handout.
- No technical explainer of how models work.
- No call-to-action close. No "three things to do Monday." No hiring or
  promotion checklist.
- No "RTW," no reading/thinking/writing framework label, no acronyms.
- No compositional-vs-directive distinction. It has been cut from this talk.

## Research (do this before writing Section 3)

Find current, accurate, citable figures for the material substrate:

- Data-center energy consumption — per-query and aggregate where available.
- Water used for cooling.
- Mineral and rare-earth extraction in the hardware supply chain.
- Training compute and its cost.
- Conditions of data-labeling and content-moderation labor.

Prefer primary and authoritative sources — peer-reviewed work, the IEA, major
investigative reporting. Capture full citations with DOIs (or stable URLs) for the
handout. **Do not put any number in the spoken text that you cannot source.** If the
evidence is contested, choose the figure you can defend and note the contest in the
handout, not from the podium.

A clean one-page handout for distribution. Organize it by the talk's themes: why we
