A lot of this labor is outsourced. A lot of it is done by people in countries where wages are low and worker protections are weak.

So when you sit down and type to this tool and get a clean, useful response in 10 seconds, behind that 10 seconds is a server that cost millions to build, infrastructure that is consuming electricity at a scale you probably have not thought about, water being used in regions where water is precious, minerals extracted under conditions most of us would find unacceptable if we let ourselves think about them, and human labor, mostly invisible, done by people we will never meet and will never compensate.

This part is going to get uncomfortable, and I want to sit with the discomfort for a minute instead of rushing past it.

When you hand off a task to a tool that is very good at that task, something happens to your capacity to do it yourself. You do not notice it immediately. It happens gradually. But it is real.

The mechanism is simple. Skill is built by doing. You do a task, you get feedback, you improve. You do it again, you get better. Over years, you build judgment. You build intuition. You build the ability to notice what is wrong and fix it.

But if you do not do the task, you do not build the skill.

And if the tool you are using is very good—if it produces clean output that is hard to find fault with—then you are not getting good feedback. You are not building judgment about what the output should look like, because the output already looks good.

This is subtle, but it is real.

Let me give you a concrete example. You are a clinician. You have been writing clinical notes for twenty years. You know what a good note looks like. You know what details matter. You know what to watch out for.

Then a tool arrives that can draft notes for you. You try it. It is fast. The notes are coherent. They hit the important points. You start using it regularly.

Five years later, what has happened? You have written fewer notes yourself. Your hand has less memory for what a note should be. You have not caught as many mistakes, because you are not reading as carefully, because you are reading text that a machine produced and you are trusting it. And your intuition about what matters in a note—that thing you built over decades—is a little more faint than it was.

This is not a catastrophic loss. It is a fade. Competence does not disappear. But it thins. And thinned competence makes you more dependent on the tool. And more dependent on the tool means you are less likely to notice when the tool makes a mistake.

This is the thing that keeps me up at night. Not the fear that machines will become intelligent and take over—that is science fiction and it distracts from what is actually happening. What is actually happening is much simpler and much harder to fight. What is actually happening is that confident output is being trusted more than it has earned the right to be. The tool produces something that looks competent, and because it looks competent, we trust it. And the more we trust it without independent judgment, the more that trust becomes a habit. A faith. A thing that no longer requires thinking.

And that is a problem, because the tool is not always right.

Sometimes it is spectacularly wrong. Sometimes it invents citations. Sometimes it describes something that never happened. Sometimes it gets the basic facts backward.

But most of the time it is close enough. Close enough that a tired person reading it quickly will not catch the error. Close enough that a person who is not expert in the domain will not know the error is there.

And when confident output is trusted without sufficient judgment, errors that should have been caught do not get caught.

In a field like health information, in a domain where the stakes are human bodies and human lives, confident output that has not earned its confidence is a problem that is not small.

I want to talk about thinking.

Not as a skill on a list. Not as something to optimize. Thinking—the plain act of working something out in your own mind. Holding a question. Turning it over. Being confused, and staying with the confusion, and slowly coming to understand. That.

Here is the thing about thinking that I keep coming back to. It is the one act that cannot be handed off without ceasing to be yours.

You can hand off the writing. The machine will produce sentences. You can hand off the search, the summary, the first draft, the calculation. All of that can be done for you, and the result can be real, and you can use it. But the moment you hand off the thinking—the actual working-out, the part where you hold the thing in your own mind and come to understand it—the moment you hand that off, it is no longer your thought. There is an answer on the screen, but you did not think it. And an answer you did not think is not knowledge that you have. It is just a thing in your possession that you are trusting. You own it the way you own a fact you read on a sign. It is not in you. You did not earn it. And when the sign is gone, you have nothing.

This is the difference between having an answer and understanding something. The machine is very good at giving you answers. It cannot give you understanding, because understanding is not a thing that can be transferred. It is not a file. It only ever forms one way: a mind does the work. There is no version of understanding that you receive without thinking. The thinking is not the means to the understanding. The thinking is the understanding. They are the same event.

And reading is part of this, more than we usually admit. We talk about reading as if it were just intake—getting the information off the page and into your head. But real reading is not intake. It is thinking, done with someone else's words as the occasion for it. When you read something hard and slow, and you stop, and you go back, and you argue with it in your head, you are not absorbing it. You are thinking it through. That is why a summary is never the same as having read the thing. The machine will summarize anything for you now—a report, an article, a whole book—and the summary will be accurate, and you will have the gist, and you will not have done the reading. You will have the conclusions without the journey that made the conclusions mean anything. And a profession that stops reading for itself, that takes the gist and skips the thinking that reading is, slowly loses the ability to tell a good argument from a bad one. Because that ability was built by reading. It does not come from the summary.

So when I say the thinking is what is at stake, I mean the reading too, and the writing too, because those are not separate from thinking. They are thinking, done on a page. When you write something out yourself—really write it, struggle with the sentence until it says what you actually mean—you usually discover that you did not fully know what you meant until you wrote it. The writing was not the recording of a finished thought. The writing was where the thought got finished. Hand that off, and you get clean sentences and an unfinished mind. The page looks done. The thinking never happened.
