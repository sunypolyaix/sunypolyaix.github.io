Reading and Writing with AI: Why Universal AI Literacy Matters for Health Information Professionals

AI is already in your workflows — and it isn't asking for your prompt.

Talk · June 9, 2026 2:15–3:15 PM · Verona, NY Emerging Trends Track
AIX Center  /  Talk
Overview

Most AI literacy training assumes you're the one prompting. You type, it responds, you decide what to do with the output. That's one kind of AI — compositional — and the training around it is well-developed. But it's not the only kind in your workflows, and for many HIM professionals it may not be the dominant kind.

Directive AI is different: the system has already acted before you engage. A suggested code. A flagged record. A risk score surfaced in your queue. No one asked you to prompt anything. The failure modes that matter here — automation bias, anchoring, fixation, overtrust — are different from the ones compositional training addresses. And the literacy required to govern them is different too.

This talk maps those two kinds of AI across the domains of HIM practice, argues that the relevant literacy requirement scales with credential level, and makes the case that for supervisors and leaders, compositional literacy is not optional — it's the prerequisite for governing the directive AI already in your organization.

Two Kinds of AI
Compositional
You ask it
You initiate. You prompt. The model responds and you work with the output — reading, revising, deciding. Your judgment forms before the AI acts.
→ Drafting a policy memo
→ Summarizing a dense document
→ Generating coding rationale
Directive
It prompts you
The AI has already acted. A recommendation surfaces before you've formed an independent view. The model's output arrives first — then you respond to it.
→ Suggested ICD code in the workflow
→ Flagged record in your queue
→ Risk score on a patient record
Literacy by Credential Level
AssociateIndividual contributor
Works primarily with directive AI embedded in clinical and administrative workflows. Needs to recognize AI-assisted outputs, flag anomalies, and know when to escalate — not to prompt from scratch.
BachelorSupervisory
Needs compositional literacy to audit what the directive AI in their unit is producing, train staff on appropriate use, and write policy around AI-assisted workflows. Cannot govern what they cannot interrogate.
MasterOrganizational leadership
Must have compositional literacy. Makes institutional decisions about AI deployment, vendor evaluation, and governance. If you want to lead in HIM, you have to have felt what the model can and can't do.

Data D · Domain I/II
Automation bias

Automation bias
The Coding Accuracy Audit
The model pattern-recognizes confidently across a discharge dataset but misses a clinical context flag that changes the principal diagnosis. The coder accepts the suggestion without reviewing the underlying documentation. Confidence without calibration.
Anchoring
Model's first framing forecloses the analysis
Anchoring
The Discharge Data
The model surfaces a regional comparison and frames it as the story. The analyst builds from that frame rather than asking whether it's the right question. The model's first interpretation becomes the ceiling of the analysis, not the floor.
Delegation
Memo reflects model's structure, not analyst's judgment
Delegation
The Quality Report Memo
The analyst hands the model a dataset and asks for a memo to leadership. The model produces something that reads well but foregrounds findings the analyst would not have prioritized. The analyst submits it without restructuring. The output is technically accurate and intellectually not theirs.
Policy P · Domain IV/V
Fixation
Model reads the federal standard; misses state variation
Fixation
The Retention Policy Gap
The model reads the HIPAA retention standard accurately and confidently — but New York State imposes a longer requirement. The model fixates on the federal floor and never surfaces the state override. The professional reads the model's output and stops there.
Anchoring
Model's legal interpretation becomes the working assumption
Anchoring
The Information Blocking Rule
The model interprets an information blocking exception as clearly applicable. The HIM professional anchors on that interpretation and drafts the policy response around it without consulting counsel. The model's reading of an ambiguous regulatory passage hardens into organizational policy.
Deskilling
Policy language becomes model-generated boilerplate
Deskilling
The Breach Notification Policy
The department has used the model to draft every policy update for two years. The staff who used to write from scratch no longer can. When a novel situation arises that the model handles poorly, no one in the department has the muscle to recognize it or correct it. The model didn't degrade the policy — it degraded the team.
Judgment J · Domain VII
Overtrust
Model summarizes a complex case; nuance disappears
Overtrust
The AI Note in the Chart
An AI-generated clinical note summarizes the patient's visit accurately in aggregate but softens a detail that matters for the coding decision. The HIM professional reads the note as authoritative and does not cross-reference the underlying documentation. The model's summary displaces the record.
Automation bias
Model hedges; professional defers rather than decides
Automation bias
The Physician Who Won't Document
The model, asked what to do about a physician who consistently fails to complete records, produces a balanced summary of options. The supervisor presents the model's analysis to leadership as a recommendation. The model didn't make a decision — and neither did the supervisor. Automation bias as abdication.
Delegation
Model drafts the response; professional signs it
Delegation
The Record Request That Worries You
A sensitive ROI request arrives. The HIM professional is uncertain and asks the model to draft a response. The model produces a technically sound letter. The professional sends it. The judgment that should have happened — whether this request is legitimate, whether counsel should be involved — was never made. The model wrote around the decision point.
