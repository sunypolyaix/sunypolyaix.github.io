Reading and Writing with AI: How We Live with a New Kind of Mind

AI is the latest in a ten-thousand-year line of cognitive augmentations. The LLM is the first one that generates.

Talk · June 9, 2026 2:15–3:15 PM · Turning Stone Emerging Trends Track
AIX Center  /  Talk
The Argument

For as long as there have been people, we've had one stubborn problem: a thought lives in one head, and it dies there, unless we can get it out. The whole history of human civilization is the history of solving that problem — finding ways to hold thought, move it, share it, work on it. Every augmentation in that line carried human thought further. Not one of them made it.

That changed on November 30, 2022. A system called ChatGPT went public, and for the first time in ten thousand years, a machine didn't just carry what we wrote — it wrote. Open-ended language, on any subject, made on the spot. Language escaped its human container. That is the genuine discontinuity. Everything else follows from it.

This talk maps where we are, names what the technology actually is, accounts for what it costs, and argues that the right posture is engagement with agency — not defense, not surrender. It ends with a personal account of what it looks like to actually live this way.

What It Actually Is

The foundational invention is transformer architecture — a technical breakthrough published in 2017. Think of it the way you think about the alphabet: a small set of principles that made a whole world of things possible. Built on top of it is a class of systems called Large Language Models — LLMs. The ones you've heard of are all built on those.

transformer architecture
Large Language Model · LLM
ChatGPT · Claude · Gemini · Copilot · Perplexity

These systems don't retrieve facts. They learn patterns — how words follow words, how ideas sit near other ideas. From that map of patterns, they produce new text that fits. The whole machine is built to produce a response, not to sit there in silence. Which means it can be completely fluent and completely wrong in exactly the same voice. The confidence isn't a signal that it's right. It's just how it talks.

The model isn't awake. It isn't sitting there thinking about you while you're away. You send it a question; it wakes up, generates a response, and goes back to sleep. There's no continuous mind on the other side. There's a system that switches on when you ask, and is otherwise still.

What It Costs

The screen is clean. The answer just appears. Underneath that clean surface is one of the heaviest physical objects we've ever made.

There is also a genuine intellectual question that hasn't been settled: the "stochastic parrots" argument holds that these systems don't understand anything — that they're sophisticated pattern-matchers stitching together plausible text with nothing underneath. This critique has real force. Whether what these systems do is a new kind of thinking, or only a convincing imitation, is a question this culture gets to argue about. We don't have to inherit the answer.

The benefits must outweigh the costs. The question of what it is doesn't, in the end, matter as much as how do we live with it.

The Same Fear, Every Time

Every augmentation on that line frightened people when it arrived. Plato worried that writing would hollow out memory and give people the appearance of wisdom in place of the real thing. He wasn't entirely wrong — something did change. But look what happened. We got science. We got law. We got medicine, and records, and this conference. The augmentation didn't hollow us out. It enlarged us.

There is one particular fear with a remarkably bad track record: the fear that a new tool for thinking will take our humanity away. That the written word will rot our memory. That the calculator will kill arithmetic. The fear is ancient. It comes back with every augmentation. And every time, we are more, not less.

new ways of augmenting our thoughts

The fear — and its track record

Two intellectual anchors: Doug Engelbart's 1962 framework Augmenting Human Intellect describes the cognitive extension project humans have always been engaged in. Ted Nelson's Computer Lib / Dream Machines (1974) imagined machines that would make people freer through computers. Both saw the augmentation line clearly — decades before anyone could see where it would go.

A Genuinely Exotic Kind of Mind

AI augments human intellect. It is not a tool. It is not a person. It is a genuinely exotic kind of mind — not human. Not pretending to be human. But able to do something no augmentation ever could: meet us in language, and generate.

People talk to it like a someone, and feel met by it — and that isn't simply a mistake, because the thing genuinely answers. A not-person that we relate to as a someone. We've never had that before. We don't even have a word for it.

a different kind of mind exotic artificial entity immigrant species neither a person nor a tool
Living With It — model in the loop

We built these things. This new kind of mind didn't arrive from somewhere else. We made it, out of our own written record, with our own hands and a staggering amount of our own resources. Which means the question of what we do with it was never really a question about the technology. It's a question about us.

The right posture: not defense, not surrender. The people who came through every earlier augmentation intact weren't the ones who refused the new thing, and they weren't the ones who let it run them. They were the ones who picked it up on purpose, learned what it actually was, and decided — for themselves — how they'd use it.
