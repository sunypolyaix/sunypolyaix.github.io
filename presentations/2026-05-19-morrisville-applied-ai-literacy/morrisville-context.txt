PART 1: THE TALK
------------------------------------------------------------

DISCLOSURE

One hundred percent of the talk's content is AI-generated -- except the prompts.
Prior knowledge of Morrisville and AI literacy informed the prompts. The details --
the aviation literature, the failure modes, the greenhouse systems -- were developed
through sustained conversation with a language model. The source transcripts are in
the repository shared at the session. The full corpus is approximately 45,000 words.
The finished talk is approximately 900 words. The ratio is not waste. It is the
evidence of composition.

SECTION 1: MOST AI LITERACY WAS BUILT FOR THE WRONG STUDENT

When most people in higher education talk about AI literacy, they mean this: a student
prompts a model, gets text back, and makes decisions about what to do with that output.
The evidence of their thinking is in the conversation. The product is a document, an
argument, a piece of writing.

That is the compositional case. Almost all of the AI literacy pedagogy being developed
right now -- the frameworks, the rubrics, the course guidelines -- was built for that case.

It does not describe what nurses, automotive technicians, dairy farmers, or greenhouse
managers encounter. For these practitioners, AI is embedded infrastructure that recommends
an action before the human has formed an independent assessment. The cognitive demand is
not revision. It is judgment at the moment of deployment.

SECTION 2: STOP ONE -- THE DAIRY FARM

Students working in automated dairy operations encounter a milking parlor where robotic
systems handle the milking. The AI monitors each animal: milk yield, conductivity, milking
frequency, body weight, reproductive status. The system makes feeding recommendations. It
flags animals for attention. It surfaces patterns that suggest a cow needs follow-up.

The student's job is not to milk the cow. It is to read the dashboard, evaluate the flag,
and decide whether to act on it.

The system has already shaped the field of attention. The student arrives after the
dashboard has decided what looks important.

The architecture underneath many modern AI systems is literally called a transformer, built
around what computer scientists call an attention mechanism: a way of weighting which signals
matter most in a given context. Humans do something similar. But now another attention system
is entering the workflow before the human operator begins evaluating the situation.

The dashboard is not the situation. It is the model's interpretation of the situation.

Under what circumstances should a student override it? That is not a technical question.
That is a literacy question.

SECTION 3: STOP TWO -- THE AUTO SHOP

Automotive technology students will work in shops where the first thing that happens when
a vehicle comes in is someone plugs in an OBD-II scanner. AI-assisted diagnostic platforms
cross-reference trouble codes against repair records for similar vehicles, group related codes,
prioritize probable causes, and present a recommended repair sequence with confidence scores.

The student sees the recommendation on a tablet. The AI has already organized what looks
important before hands-on inspection begins. The workflow is: recommendation first,
verification second -- if at all.

What does it take to recognize when the recommendation is wrong? That is not a scanning
skill. That is a judgment skill.

SECTION 4: STOP THREE -- THE GREENHOUSE

Horticulture students will manage crops in environments where AI is already making autonomous
decisions. Irrigation systems adjust water delivery without asking anyone. Climate control
systems set temperature, humidity, and CO2 levels. Camera systems scan for disease and pest
presence, flag anomalies, and recommend treatments.

The grower receives a report. An alert. A dashboard. The system has often already acted
before they see it.

What does fixation look like when the disease alert fires and attention goes to the flagged
plant while the actual outbreak is two rows over? What does it cost when the dashboard
declares the field healthy and the student stops looking?

In each of the three cases, the cognitive challenge is the same: the AI system increasingly
organizes what the operator notices first.

SECTION 5: THE COGNITIVE CHALLENGE

Human-factors researchers have documented recurring failure modes when operators work
alongside automated systems. These appear in aviation, clinical decision support, and
process control:

1. Manual skill atrophy -- procedural skills degrade from disuse.
2. Plan continuation bias -- cognitive commitment to the AI-established course despite new cues.
3. Vigilance decrement -- attention drifts from primary indicators during automation.
4. Omission errors -- failing to notice when the automation misses something.
5. Commission errors -- acting on automated recommendations without independent verification.
6. Confirmation bias -- misinterpreting ambiguous information to confirm the original plan.
7. Expectation-driven processing -- seeing what the AI predicted, not what is actually there.

What these have in common is not complexity. They are all failures of attention. The
operator's attention has been pre-organized by the system, and independent judgment has been
displaced by cue-following.

Aviation addressed several of these through mandatory manual flying and scenario-based
training under realistic conditions. The FAA can mandate that because the stakes are
existential -- plane crashes kill people.

Students at Morrisville are not pilots. The claim is not that they need to train for
manual backup. The claim is narrower: they need to maintain an independent model of the
situation -- to recognize when their own observation of the cow, the car, or the plant
contradicts what the system is telling them, and to know what to do with that contradiction.

That is a practiced habit of judgment. It does not emerge from instruction about AI.

SECTION 6: WHAT MAKES THIS ROUND DIFFERENT

Many earlier automation failures were mechanically legible. The pump breaks. The sensor
malfunctions. You can see it. You can trace it.

This round of AI-assisted automation is different in three ways.

First, the logic is opaque. The system makes a recommendation based on patterns learned
from large amounts of data. You cannot always ask it why and get a clear answer.

Second, it fails by being confidently wrong. AI can produce a plausible-looking
recommendation that is incorrect for a specific cow, vehicle, or crop -- and nothing in
the interface signals that.

Third, introducing AI into automation may actually increase the cognitive demand on the
human operator. The milking robot reduced physical labor. The AI-assisted milking system
requires constantly evaluating recommendations from a system the operator cannot fully see
into, at scale, every day.

The issue is not simply that AI produces recommendations. The issue is that AI systems
increasingly organize what the operator notices first -- before the operator has had a
chance to form an independent assessment.

SECTION 7: WHAT AI LITERACY MEANS HERE

Not: teach students to use the tools.
Not: teach students about automation bias.

This: design courses where students practice the moment of judgment before compliance.
Where the AI recommendation is sometimes wrong by design. Where they have to articulate
why they are pausing. Where following the system blindly costs them something in
simulation before it costs them something in the field.

Students need to learn to recognize the dashboard as model-generated rather than
reality-generated. That is a different habit than spotting hallucinations in a text.
It is the habit of maintaining epistemic separation between what the model surfaced
and what is actually happening in the barn, the shop, or the greenhouse.

Dairy students should encounter scenarios where the feeding algorithm has miscalculated
and they have to catch it by reading the herd. Auto students should encounter vehicles
where the scanner recommendation is wrong and hands-on inspection is the only thing that
catches it. Horticulture students should encounter disease alerts that are false positives --
and silent outbreaks the system missed.

That is not a new course. That is a design principle that can be embedded in courses
already being taught.

AI literacy in applied fields is not primarily about producing content with AI. It is
about preserving independent professional judgment while AI systems increasingly organize
what appears important before humans even begin to think.

The question is not whether students can use AI. It is whether they can maintain an
independent model of the situation while AI is already organizing what they see.

PART 2: THE FRAMEWORK -- RTW
------------------------------------------------------------

RTW (Reading, Thinking, Writing) is the analytical spine of this AI literacy work.

AI literacy is reading, thinking, and writing using tools, techniques, and technological
systems consistent with one's peers. This is a descriptive-analytical definition, not a
normative one. It treats AI as cognitive infrastructure, not as a special object requiring
critical competency development separate from the discipline.

The key distinction: compositional AI use versus directive AI use.

Compositional AI use: the student issues a prompt, receives text, revises, iterates.
The student is the author. The conversation is the work. The artifact is evidence the
work concluded.

Directive AI use: an embedded autonomous system makes recommendations or takes actions
that the human then evaluates and executes. The student is an operator, not a composer.
The literacy challenge is different: it is not about shaping output but about maintaining
independent situational judgment while the system has already organized the field of attention.

Current AI literacy pedagogy -- including most frameworks now circulating in higher education --
was built for the compositional case. It fails applied-program students who encounter AI as
autonomous infrastructure.

The transcript is the work. Grading only the artifact is invalid -- it captures only that
the work concluded, not how it was conducted. The transcript captures the full arc of
authorship: the prompts, the pushback, the iteration, the judgment exercised at each step.

PART 3: THE LEARNER'S PERMIT (LP)
------------------------------------------------------------

The Learner's Permit is a 15-exercise content-neutral scaffold for AI literacy.
It runs in any discipline. The instructor replaces [TOPIC] with a specific subject.

Three phases:
  Phase I -- Learning to Drive (Exercises 1-5): first conversations, compression,
             pushback, trust audit, sustained conversation.
  Phase II -- Building Skill (Exercises 6-10): multi-model work, audience analysis,
              reading with AI, source comparison, workbench organization.
  Phase III -- Independent Operation (Exercises 11-15): self-directed learning,
               synthesis document, self-quiz, public-facing writing, exam.

Exercise 15 is the exam. There is no separate examination event. A General AI License
is awarded when Exercise 15 is accepted by an instructor.

Core practices (RTW in the LP context):
  Read -- read what the model generates with attention. Reading is bidirectional.
  Think -- evaluate, push back, extend. Thinking happens in the conversation.
  Write -- prompting is writing. A prompt causes the model to generate toward an outcome.

The workbench: students build a folder structure (transcripts, artifacts, synthesis,
author, zip) that persists across courses. The /author folder contains only documents
written without a model open. The workbench is portable and model-agnostic.

The artifact is the model's final response, shaped by the student's prompting. The
transcript is the full conversation. Both are the work. Both are submitted.

Key LP moves:
  Exercise 3: Push Back -- find a claim to challenge. Note whether the model defends
              its position or concedes because of social pressure. Agreeable is not
              the same as accurate.
  Exercise 4: Trust Audit -- ask the model where not to trust it. Evaluate whether
              its self-assessment matches the skepticism developed in earlier exercises.
  Exercise 12: Synthesis Document -- a portable compressed overview of what has been
               learned, designed to be uploaded to any model as opening context.
  Exercise 13: Did You Learn Your Own Slop? -- take a quiz generated from your own
               artifacts. Test whether you learned what you generated, or generated
               without learning.

Prerequisite for CDL enrollment: General AI License plus a portfolio of workbench
evidence from at least one additional course beyond the LP course.

------------------------------------------------------------
PART 4: THE COMMERCIAL LICENSE (CDL)
------------------------------------------------------------

The Commercial License certifies supervised agentic operation in controlled environments.
It is the next credential after the General AI License.

Credential progression:
  1. Learner's Permit (Exercises 1-14)
  2. General AI License (pass Exercise 15 of the LP)
  3. CDL Permit (enroll in CDL course -- requires portfolio review)
  4. Commercial License (pass Exercise 15 of the CDL course)

The CDL distinction: the LP trains compositional AI use -- prompt, receive, revise.
The CDL trains agentic operation -- the student directs an AI agent (Claude Code) that
reads files, writes code, runs commands, and acts across multiple steps without
step-by-step instruction.

RTW upgraded for agentic contexts:
  Read -- read the agent's action trace, not just its output. What did it do step by step?
          What did it decide without being asked?
  Think -- evaluate the agent's decisions, not just its results. A good result from a bad
           process is still a bad process.
  Write -- writing now means the task brief: a scoped document specifying what the agent
           may do, what it may not do, and what success looks like. Written before any
           run begins.

Three deliverables per CDL exercise: artifact, trace (execution log), transcript. All three
are the work.

The Failure Classification Template: every failure -- crash, silent error, spec mismatch,
scope violation -- is documented before briefing the agent to fix it. Silent errors are
always High severity. A silent error looks correct and is not. It is more dangerous than
a crash because it is invisible.

The project: all CDL exercises build toward a single deliverable -- a syllabus management
app that ingests course syllabi, extracts deadlines, and generates cross-course reminders.

The Pre-Trip Inspection Protocol: a checklist completed before every agent session. No run
starts without it. Built in Exercise 4 and used from Exercise 5 forward.

PART 5: KEY CLAIMS AND THEIR LIMITS
------------------------------------------------------------

What this framework asserts:

1. Most AI literacy pedagogy fails applied-program students because it was designed for
   the compositional case. Students who will encounter AI as directive infrastructure --
   systems that recommend action before the human has formed an independent view -- need
   different preparation.

2. The relevant failure modes are documented in human-factors research (aviation, clinical
   decision support, process control). They are not speculative.

3. The pedagogical response is scenario-based practice with consequential stakes in
   simulation -- not instruction about bias, not tool tutorials.

4. The transcript is assessment evidence. Grading only the artifact misses the authorship.

5. AI literacy is not a separate competency to be developed in an AI course. It is a
   dimension of disciplinary practice.

What this framework does not assert:

1. Students in applied programs need to be able to perform AI functions manually as backup.
   The aviation analogy supports maintaining independent situational judgment, not mandatory
   manual skill maintenance. The stakes are categorically different from aviation.

2. AI systems are generally unreliable. The claim is specific: AI fails confidently and
   opaquely, and the failure mode differs from mechanical failure in ways that require
   deliberate preparation.

3. This framework is complete. The CDL addresses agentic use cases that the LP does not.
   Neither addresses every applied context.
