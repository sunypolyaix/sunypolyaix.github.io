Appendix 2: Background of the Framework Development
The Information Literacy Competency Standards for Higher Education were published in 2000 and brought information literacy into higher education conversations and advanced our field. These, like all ACRL standards, are reviewed cyclically. In July 2011, ACRL appointed a Task Force to decide what, if anything, to do with the then-current Standards. In June 2012, the Task Force recommended that the Standards be significantly revised. This previous review Task Force made recommendations that informed the 2013 revision Task Force, with the following charge:
to update the Information Literacy Competency Standards for Higher Education so they reflect the current thinking on such things as the creation and dissemination of knowledge, the changing global higher education and learning environment, the shift from information literacy to information fluency, and the expanding definition of information literacy to include multiple literacies, for example, transliteracy, media literacy, digital literacy, etc.
The Task Force released the first version of the Framework in two parts in February and April of 2014 and received comments via two online hearings and a feedback form available online for four weeks. The committee then revised the document, released the second draft on June 17, 2014, and sought extensive feedback through a feedback form, two online hearings, an in-person hearing, and analysis of social media and topical blog posts.
On a regular basis, the Task Force used all of ACRL’s and American Library Association’s (ALA) communication channels to reach individual members and ALA and ACRL units (committees, sections, round tables, cultural affinity groups, chapters, and divisions) with updates. The Task Force’s liaison at ACRL maintained a private e-mail distribution list of over 1,300 individuals who attended a fall, spring, or summer online forum; provided comments to the February, April, June, or November drafts; or were otherwise identified as having a strong interest and expertise. This included members of the Task Force that drafted the Standards, leading Library Information Science (LIS) researchers and national project directors, members of the Information Literacy Rubric Development Team for the Association of American Colleges & Universities, and the Valid Assessment of Learning in Undergraduate Education initiative. Via all these channels, the Task Force regularly shared updates, invited discussion at virtual and in-person forums and hearings, and encouraged comments on public drafts of the proposed Framework.
ACRL recognized early on that the effect of any changes to the Standards would be significant within the library profession and in higher education more broadly. In addition to general announcements, the Task Force contacted nearly 60 researchers who cited the Standards in publications outside LIS literature, more than 70 deans, associate deans, directors or chairs of LIS schools, and invited specific staff leaders (and press or communications contacts) at more than 70 other higher education associations, accrediting agencies, and library associations and consortia to encourage their members to read and comment on the draft.
The Task Force systematically reviewed feedback from the first and second drafts of the Framework, including comments, criticism, and praise provided through formal and informal channels. The three official online feedback forms had 562 responses; numerous direct e-mails were sent to members of the Task Force. The group was proactive in tracking feedback on social media, namely blog posts and Twitter. While the data harvested from social media are not exhaustive, the Task Force made its best efforts to include all known Twitter conversations, blog posts, and blog commentary. In total, there were several hundred feedback documents, totaling over a thousand pages, under review. The content of these documents was analyzed by members of the Task Force and coded using HyperResearch, a qualitative data analysis software. During the drafting and vetting process, the Task Force provided more detail on the feedback analysis in an online FAQ document.
The Task Force continued to revise the document and published the third revision in November 2014, again announcing broadly and seeking comments via a feedback form.

2024-2026 Revision
The ACRL Framework for Information Literacy for Higher Education Review and Revision Task Force conducted a comprehensive review of the Framework beginning in July 2024 and ending in June 2026.  
The Task Force analyzed the existing Framework and companion documents and examined current literature, focusing on strengths and opportunities for improvement. To gather broad stakeholder input, the Task Force developed and deployed a survey and conducted focus groups both virtually and in-person at the 2025 ACRL Conference. The survey received 371 responses. Focus groups included 17 in-person participants and 86 virtual participants representing a range of colleges and universities in the United States and Canada.
The Task Force reviewed and coded the survey and focus group data to identify emerging themes. These themes indicate elements participants and respondents felt were important in some way, signaling helpful existing aspects as well as concepts they felt could potentially be added or improved upon. Themes from both the survey and focus group data include: generative AI, critical information literacy, approachable language and style, pedagogy and practice, inclusion and social justice, supplementary documentation (discipline-specific, communication guidance/outreach), and specific feedback for frames.
The Task Force then reviewed all gathered data and employed qualitative analysis to identify key and emerging themes and revision priorities. After reviewing the collected evidence and holding numerous discussions as a full committee, the group drafted a revised Framework that was expanded to address emerging technologies, critical approaches, and evolving information literacy needs in today's higher education landscape.

Appendix 1: Integrating the Framework to Support Information Literacy Across Campus
The Framework is a mechanism for guiding the development of information literacy programs within higher education institutions while also promoting discussion about the nature of key concepts in information in general education and disciplinary studies. The Framework encourages thinking about how librarians, faculty, and others can address core or threshold concepts and associated elements in the information field within the context of higher education. The Framework will help librarians contextualize and integrate information literacy for their institutions and will encourage a deeper understanding of what knowledge practices and dispositions an information literate student should develop. The Framework delineates the boundaries of what librarians teach and how they conceptualize the study of information within the curricula of higher education institutions.
The Framework has been conceived as a set of living documents on which the profession builds. The key product is a set of frames through which to view information literacy, each of which includes a concept central to information literacy, knowledge practices, and dispositions. The Association of College & Research Libraries (ACRL) encourages the library community to discuss the Framework widely and to develop resources to supplement the core set of materials in the frames.
The frames can guide the redesign of information literacy programs for general education courses, for upper level courses in students’ major departments, and for graduate student education. The frames are intended to illustrate evolving degrees of complexity or nuance in approaches to information over a learning journey in specific areas; movement may take place throughout a student’s academic career as well as continuing throughout a lifetime . Mapping out in what way specific concepts will be integrated into specific curriculum levels is one of the challenges of implementing the Framework. ACRL encourages librarians to work with faculty, departmental or college curriculum committees, instructional designers, staff from centers for teaching and learning, and others to design information literacy programs in a holistic way.
ACRL realizes that many information literacy librarians currently meet with students via one-shot classes, especially in introductory level classes. Over the course of a student’s academic program, one-shot sessions that address a particular need at a particular time, systematically integrated into the curriculum, can play a significant role in an information literacy program. It is important for librarians and teaching faculty to understand that the Framework is not designed to be implemented in a single information literacy session in a student’s academic career; it is intended to be developmentally and systematically integrated into the student’s academic program at a variety of levels. This may take considerable time to implement fully in many institutions.
ACRL encourages information literacy librarians to be imaginative and innovative in implementing the Framework in their institution. The Framework is not intended to be prescriptive but to be used as a guidance document in shaping an institutional program. ACRL recommends piloting the implementation of the Framework in a context that is useful to a specific institution, assessing the results and sharing experiences with colleagues.
Introduction for Disciplinary Faculty and Administrators
Considering Information Literacy
Information literacy is the set of integrated abilities encompassing the reflective discovery of information, the understanding of how information is produced and valued, and the use of information in creating new knowledge and participating ethically in communities of learning.
This Framework sets forth these information literacy concepts and describes how librarians as information professionals can facilitate the development of information literacy by postsecondary students.
Creating a Framework
ACRL has played a leading role in promoting information literacy in higher education for decades. The Information Literacy Competency Standards for Higher Education (Standards), first published in 2000, enabled colleges and universities to position information literacy as an essential learning outcome in the curriculum and promoted linkages with general education programs, service learning, problem-based learning, and other pedagogies focused on deeper learning. Regional accrediting bodies, the American Association of Colleges and Universities (AAC&U), and various discipline-specific organizations employed and adapted the Standards.
In light of changes in higher education, coupled with increasingly complex information ecosystems, the Framework seeks to address the great potential for information literacy as a deeper, more integrated learning agenda, addressing academic and technical courses, undergraduate research, community-based learning, and co-curricular learning experiences of entering freshmen through graduation. Searching as Strategic Exploration
Searching for information is often nonlinear and iterative, requiring the use and evaluation of a range of information sources and the mental flexibility to pursue alternate avenues as new understanding develops throughout the lifecycle of the project.
Searches use a range of methods and resources to obtain relevant sources, most often through databases, online search engines, and other platforms. The act of searching often begins with a question that directs the act of finding needed information. Searchers use a variety of methods to identify and obtain relevant sources. Approaches to searching can vary in complexity depending on the searcher’s skills and information need. The experience of searching is impacted by several internal and external factors, including cognitive, affective, cultural, social, economic, and political dimensions. More experienced learners realize that information searching is a complex process, in which they utilize multiple search strategies to identify sources depending on the scope and context of their information need. Less experienced learners may use fewer search strategies to search a more limited set of resources before developing more advanced skills.

Learners who are developing their information literate abilities
determine the initial scope of the task and types of sources that could address their information needs;
match information needs to appropriate search tools and information producers;
recognize that information systems may incorporate metadata, algorithms, rankings, user engagement, artificial intelligence, or obscure processes to display and curate search results;
utilize divergent thinking (e.g., brainstorming) and consider using potentially relevant resources such as knowledgeable individuals or artificial intelligence tools to explore and expand on ideas around the information need;  
distinguish between different types of searching language (e.g., controlled vocabulary, keywords, natural language) and use the most effective method for the search;
acknowledge that search terms change over time based on the historical and social context of the topics being explored;
utilize convergent thinking (e.g., selecting useful sources) when searching, including using identified sources to connect to other potential sources;
devise and refine search strategies as necessary, based on available tools, search results and the searcher’s evolving information need;
identify stakeholders, such as scholars and other individuals, communities, cultures, organizations, governments, and industries, who might produce relevant information about a topic, and then use that knowledge to inform search strategies;
manage searching processes and results effectively by using appropriate tools or methods to document, save, and organize sources and citations;
utilize evaluation strategies to determine relevance, quality, and reliability of information sources;
revisit and adjust searching strategies as needed and acknowledge that useful information can also come from inquiry, discovery, and/or serendipity.

Knowledge Systems as Conversation (Scholarship as Conversation)
Communities of scholars, researchers, specialists or professionals engage in sustained discourse with new insights and discoveries occurring over time as a result of varied perspectives and interpretations.
The metaphor of conversation is used to describe how systems of knowledge evolve through the ways information and its creators relate to one another. Research in scholarly and professional fields is a specific type of discursive practice, or conversation, in which ideas are formulated, debated, and weighed against one another over extended periods of time in different formats and venues.  Instead of seeking discrete answers to complex problems, learners understand that a given issue may be characterized by several competing perspectives as part of an ongoing conversation in which information users and creators come together and negotiate meaning. Learners understand that, while some topics have established answers through this process, a query may not have a single uncontested answer. Learners are therefore inclined to seek out many perspectives, not merely the ones with which they are familiar. These perspectives might be within their own sphere of knowledge, culture, discipline, or profession or may be in unfamiliar areas or other fields or professions or may be in other fields. While learners at all levels can take part in the conversation, established power and authority structures may influence their ability to participate and can privilege certain voices and information. 
Developing familiarity with the sources of evidence, methods, and modes of discourse in their context, culture, or that assist emerging learners to enter the conversation. New forms of informal, scholarly and professional conversations provide more avenues in which a wide variety of voices (human and computer-generated ) may become a part of the conversation.  Evaluating credibility and providing attribution to relevant previous research, or other sources  is also an obligation of participation in the conversation. It enables the conversation to move forward and strengthens one’s voice.

Research as Inquiry
Research is iterative and depends upon asking increasingly complex or new questions resulting in answers that in turn develop additional questions or lines of inquiry in any context.
Inquiry is a process that is not limited to the academic world, and can focus on open or unresolved problems or questions to personal, professional, or societal needs. Learners recognize that extending knowledge, whether within a discipline or non-academic context, requires a collaborative efforts. Many times, this process includes points of disagreement where debate and dialogue work to deepen the conversations around knowledge. As learners acquire increasingly strategic perspectives on inquiry and a greater repertoire of investigative methods, they become better equipped to fulfill information needs more efficiently, lowering their cognitive load.
In any research situation, learners cultivate curiosity and understand that simple questions can evolve into more advanced research that explores gaps in knowledge. Through practice, learners come to understand how original research is created through human-led inquiry. Inquiry encompasses a wide spectrum, typically beginning with information gathering and simple research questions that rely on a basic restatement of existing knowledge. Learners progress toward more advanced capabilities, including the development of sophisticated research questions, the application of complex research methods, and the exploration of a greater variety of disciplinary perspectives.

Information Has Value
Information is situated within a larger knowledge system and is valuable in how learners negotiate and understand the world. Information possesses several dimensions of value including as a means of education, as a means to influence, and as a commodity. Legal and socioeconomic interests influence information production and dissemination.
The value of information is manifested in various contexts, including but not limited to accepted methods for producing knowledge, publishing practices, access to information, the commodification of personal information, and intellectual property laws. Learners may struggle to understand the different values and dimensions of information if they have not encountered situations where certain information is not plentiful or free and the concept of intellectual property is first encountered through rules of citation or warnings about plagiarism and copyright law. As creators and users of information, learners begin to understand their rights and responsibilities when participating in a knowledge community, understanding how the roles of emerging information technologies change the environment of knowledge production which has historically been a human process. 
Learners begin to recognize that the contextual value of information changes to reflect the volatile nature of a changing society, including changing views of facts and truth, changes in the publishing industry, decreased levels of public trust for information authorities and experts, the commercialization of personal data, and new information technologies that generate information. Value may be wielded by powerful forces in ways that marginalize certain voices or present disinformation as truth. However, value may also be leveraged by individuals and organizations to effect change and for civic, economic, social, or personal gains. Learners discover that the individual is responsible for making deliberate and informed choices about when to comply with and when to contest current legal, structural, socioeconomic, cultural and other practices concerning the value of information.

Information Creation as a Process
Information in any format is produced to convey a message and shared via a selected delivery method. Information can be static and complete, or dynamic and evolving. The iterative processes of researching, creating, revising, and disseminating information vary, and the resulting product reflects these differences. The information creation process refers to examining ways in which a piece of information came to be as well as the way the researcher/learner will be using the information.
 Information creation processes could result in a range of information formats and modes of delivery, so learners look beyond format when selecting resources which meet their needs. The unique capabilities and constraints of each person’s creation process as well as the specific information needed determine how the resulting product is used. Learners recognize that information creation are valued differently in different contexts, such as academia, the workplace or everyday life. Elements that shape information creation, such as a pre- or post-publication editing or reviewing process, may be indicators of quality. The dynamic nature of information creation and dissemination requires ongoing attention to understand evolving creation processes. Recognizing the nature of information creation, learners look to the underlying processes of creation as well as the subsequent product to critically evaluate the usefulness of the information. Learners begin to recognize the significance of the creation process, leading them to increasingly sophisticated choices when matching information products with their information needs.

Authority Is Constructed and Contextual
Information resources reflect their creators’ expertise, credibility, and the context in which the information is created. Resources are evaluated based on the information need and the context in which the information will be used. Authority is constructed; this means that various communities may recognize different types of authority when seeking or encountering information. It is also contextual; this means that the information need of the individual or community may help to determine the form of authority required to fulfill that need.
Learners come to understand that authority is a type of influence recognized or exerted within a community. Learners develop increasing familiarity with a field and view authority with an attitude of informed skepticism. They are open to new perspectives, to additional voices, to reviewing their own biases (including emotional reactions to information), and to changes in schools of thought. Learners know how to seek authoritative voices and understand that unrecognized and historically underrepresented voices can be authoritative depending on the context or information need. Introduction 
History of the Framework
The Framework for Information Literacy for Higher Education (Framework) positions  information literacy (IL) as an educational reform movement with a foundation in a rich, complex set of core ideas. Throughout the evolution of information literacy as a distinct concept, academic librarians and their partners in higher education have historically developed learning outcomes, tools, and resources that institutions have deployed to scaffold information literacy concepts and skills within their curricula. The publication of the Framework in 2015, with its focus on threshold concepts, enabled the expansion of information literacy’s reach. The Framework provides ideas and vocabulary to express core dispositions that cross disciplinary boundaries while enabling deep exploration of disciplinary information literacy knowledge practices. The Framework serves as a catalyst for dynamic and varied partnerships between librarians, disciplinary faculty, and administrators around information literacy, and opens the way for considering IL’s role in concepts such as analytical and integrated thinking, critical practice, inclusion, civic engagement, communication practices, and sustainability.
Information Literacy in the Current Environment
As higher education undergoes rapid and unpredictable change, our dynamic information ecosystems also continue to evolve. The advent of emerging technologies such as artificial intelligence (AI) has reshaped how we interact with information, thus requiring a reconsideration of what constitutes meaningful and ethical engagement with information. Educators are faced with a critical challenge to balance established practices with the practical, ethical, and pedagogical impacts of emerging technologies, digital and media literacy, and civic and community engagement. As traditional boundaries between knowledge creation processes and resulting information outputs become blurred, a need is emerging to expand traditional concepts like scholarship, research, and authority.
Along with the changes in information ecosystems, our understanding of how people interact with and learn about these complex systems is also evolving. More inclusive pedagogical shifts are needed to accurately reflect diverse learner realities and lifelong learning. We recognize that learning about information does not happen on a rigid novice-to-expert continuum, nor does learning begin with a blank slate and end when one has achieved “mastery.” Learning about information is a journey shaped by diverse experiences, contexts, and information needs where learners develop an increasingly complex and nuanced relationship to information along the way. Learners continue to develop as active participants in creating and disseminating knowledge, in understanding and navigating the contours and changing dynamics of the world of information, and in creating and using information, data, and scholarship ethically. 
This more expansive view of the individual learning journey also requires an acknowledgement of diverse knowledge systems and cultural contexts that shape how information is created and shared. There are many distinct cultural ways of knowing that enrich our collective understanding. We also recognize that the conceptualization of research includes the workplace and everyday life in addition to traditional academic settings. We emphasize that information literacy is essential across all contexts where people seek, evaluate, and use information to make decisions, solve problems, and participate in their communities, in addition to academic work.
In higher education, where learners are asked to navigate complex information environments, information literacy makes for an ideal thread which can be woven across interdisciplinary contexts. IL can highlight unique approaches to information within disciplines and professions as well as ask critical questions about the creation and dissemination of knowledge within those fields. Teaching faculty can design curricula and assignments that foster enhanced engagement with core ideas about information, scholarship, and critical thinking both within and across disciplines. Librarians can identify core ideas within their own knowledge domain that can enhance learning for students, help create cohesive curricula for information literacy, and collaborate more extensively with teaching faculty in teaching, learning, and curriculum planning. Librarians can also play a pivotal role in connecting information literacy to broader academic learning goals through approaches such as critical pedagogy.  
Theoretical Background of the Framework
The Framework is intentionally called a framework because it is based on a cluster of interconnected core concepts, with flexible options for implementation, rather than on a set of standards or learning outcomes, or any prescriptive enumeration of skills. At the heart of this Framework are conceptual understandings that organize many other concepts and ideas about information, research, and scholarship into a coherent whole. These conceptual understandings are informed by the work of Wiggins and McTighe, which focuses on essential concepts and questions in developing curricula, and also by threshold concepts developed by Meyer and Land. Threshold concepts can be thought of as portals through which the learner must pass in order to develop new perspectives and wider understanding.  Threshold concepts are core or foundational concepts that, once grasped by the learner, create new perspectives and ways of understanding a discipline or challenging a knowledge domain. Such concepts produce transformation within the learner; without them, the learner may not progress in that field of knowledge. The positioning of the Frames as threshold concepts for information literacy draws primarily from the work of Townsend, Hofer, Brunetti, and Hanick . Additionally, threshold concepts reinforce information literacy teaching and learning as an intentional and equitable practice. 
The Framework also draws significantly upon the concept of metaliteracy, namely from the work of Mackey and Jacobson,  which expands the scope of traditional information skills (determine, access, locate, understand, produce, and use information) to include the collaborative production and sharing of information in participatory digital environments. This approach requires an ongoing adaptation to emerging technologies such as AI, and an understanding of the critical thinking and reflection required to engage in these spaces as producers, collaborators, and distributors. Metaliteracy addresses behavioral, affective, cognitive, and metacognitive engagement with the information ecosystem. Metacognition is an awareness and understanding of one’s own thought processes which focus on how people learn and process information, taking into consideration people’s awareness of how they learn. This type of critical self-reflection is crucial to becoming more self-directed in a rapidly changing information ecosystem. 
Information Literacy: Definition and Framework Structure
DEFINITION of Information Literacy
Information literacy is the set of integrated abilities encompassing the reflective discovery of information, the understanding of how information is produced and valued, and the use of information in creating new knowledge and participating ethically in communities of learning. 
STRUCTURE of the Framework
The Framework is organized into six frames, each consisting of a concept central to information literacy, a set of knowledge practices, and a set of dispositions.
FRAMES are the threshold concepts which provide ways to frame core ideas in information literacy teaching and learning
The six concepts that anchor the frames are presented alphabetically and do not suggest a particular sequence or hierarchy in which they must be learned.
Authority Is Constructed and Contextual
Information Creation as a Process
Information Has Value
Research as Inquiry
Scholarship as Conversation
Searching as Strategic Exploration
KNOWLEDGE PRACTICES are the proficiencies or abilities that learners develop as a result of their comprehending a threshold concept. Knowledge practices serve as demonstrations of ways in which learners increase their understanding of information literacy concepts.
DISPOSITIONS are a tendency to act or think in a particular way. More specifically, a disposition is a cluster of preferences, attitudes, and intentions, as well as a set of capabilities that address the affective, attitudinal, or valuing dimensions of learning.
Neither the knowledge practices nor the dispositions that support each concept are intended to prescribe what local institutions should do in using the Framework; each library and its partners on campus will need to deploy these frames to best fit their own situation, including designing learning outcomes. For the same reason, these lists should not be considered exhaustive.
Terms used:
Learner : any person who is developing information literacy practices. We refer to learners as developing or evolving along a continuum of increasing complexity or nuance in relation to information. 
Research: an expansive term which encompasses a range of inquiry, discovery, and knowledge creation activities from a simple act of seeking out information to creating original empirical research.
Conclusion
The Framework provides a foundation for librarians, teaching faculty, and other institutional partners to design instruction , assignments, courses, and curricula; to connect information literacy with student success initiatives; to collaborate on pedagogical research and involve students in that research; to address the integration of emerging trends and technologies such as AI and digital literacy; to foster lifelong learning, ethical, civic, and community engagement with information, and to create wider conversations about learning, faculty development, the scholarship of teaching and learning, and the assessment of learning on local campuses and beyond.
