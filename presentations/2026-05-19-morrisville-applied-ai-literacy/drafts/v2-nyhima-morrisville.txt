TITLE

AI Literacy in Applied Contexts: When the AI Tells You What to Do


DESCRIPTION (100 words)

Most AI literacy frameworks assume people are writing with AI: prompting, revising, evaluating generated text. That is the compositional case. It is not the environment most healthcare information professionals work in. In practice, AI often appears as embedded infrastructure that recommends a code, flags a discrepancy, or prioritizes a risk. The cognitive demand is not composition but judgment under deployment conditions. The dominant failure modes change accordingly: automation bias, anchoring, fixation, and overtrust. This session introduces a framework for directive AI contexts and argues that AI literacy in these settings emerges less from instruction than from repeated practice under realistic conditions.

400-WORD DRAFT

That is the compositional case. It doesn't describe your work.

Most AI literacy in higher education was built for one scenario. A student prompts a model. The model returns text. The student shapes it — revises, argues, edits. The transcript of that conversation is evidence of thinking. The document at the end is evidence the work concluded.

That framework is appropriate for a lot of what happens in academic settings. It is insufficient for healthcare information management.

What HIMA professionals encounter is different. The AI is embedded in the workflow before you sit down. It has already recommended a code, flagged a discrepancy, suggested a DRG. You arrive at the output, not the conversation. And the cognitive demand isn't revision — it's a judgment call in the space between what the system said and what you sign.

That distinction matters because the failure modes change. In compositional contexts — academic writing, legal drafting, analysis — the main cognitive risk is delegation: you stop revising and accept the first draft. The cost is a weaker document. In directive contexts — coding, nursing, manufacturing — the risks are different. Automation bias: defaulting to the AI recommendation over your own clinical read. Fixation: attention locked on the AI-flagged code while a complication two lines up goes unread. Anchoring: the first recommendation distorts every subsequent check even when you think you're verifying independently. And what aviation researchers call plan continuation bias: staying on the AI-established course even when emerging cues in the record are telling you to reconsider.

The automation bias literature is well-developed. Aviation, clinical decision support, human factors — decades of research on these failure modes. What is not well-developed is the pedagogy. The AI literacy frameworks in use today assume the compositional case. They teach prompting, critical evaluation of generated text, revision strategies. These are real skills. They do not address the moment a coder pauses before accepting a suggested code, or doesn't.

That moment — the gap between AI recommendation and human decision — is where literacy in directive contexts lives. And it develops through practice, not instruction. Through repeated exposure to realistic scenarios where the decision has stakes. Through debriefs that name what fixation and plan continuation feel like from the inside.

That is what this session is about.
