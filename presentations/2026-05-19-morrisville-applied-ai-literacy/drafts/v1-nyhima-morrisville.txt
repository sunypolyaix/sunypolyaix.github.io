TITLE

AI Literacy in Applied Contexts: When the AI Tells You What to Do


DESCRIPTION (100 words)

Most AI literacy pedagogy was built for one scenario: a student prompts a model, receives text, and shapes the output. That is the compositional case. It does not describe what nurses, coders, mechanics, or agricultural technicians encounter. For these practitioners, AI is embedded infrastructure that recommends an action. The cognitive demand is not revision — it is judgment at the moment of deployment. The failure modes are different: automation bias, fixation, anchoring, perseveration. This session introduces a framework for AI literacy in directive contexts, identifies the specific cognitive errors practitioners are most likely to make, and argues that literacy emerges through practice, not instruction.

400-WORD DRAFT

I want to start by asking you two questions. The first: how transformative do you think AI will be — for healthcare, for the world? The second: how concerned are you about the long-term risks? High transformation, low risk. High transformation, high risk. Low transformation, low risk. Low transformation, high risk. Four different positions. All of them are partly right.

Here's what I find interesting. Most AI literacy in higher education — and most guidance organizations are giving to professionals right now — assumes you're in the first category. That you're using AI as a writing partner. You prompt a model, you get text back, you revise it. The evidence of your thinking is in the conversation. The artifact is the document you produce.

That is the compositional case. It doesn't describe your work.

What HIMA professionals encounter is different. The AI is embedded in your workflow before you sit down. It has already recommended a code, flagged a discrepancy, suggested a DRG. You arrive at the output, not the conversation. And the cognitive demand isn't revision — it's a judgment call in the space between what the system said and what you sign.

That distinction matters because the failure modes are different. In compositional contexts — academic writing, legal drafting, business analysis — the main cognitive risk is delegation: you stop revising and accept the first draft. Recoverable. In directive contexts — coding, nursing, manufacturing — the risks are automation bias, fixation, anchoring, and what I call perseveration: continuing down the path the AI established even when the patient, the record, or the situation is telling you something else.

The research literature has almost nothing on pedagogy for directive contexts. The frameworks are built for the compositional case.

What that means practically: the habits that protect an academic writer using AI don't protect a coder using AI. A coder needs to practice the moment of pause before acting. Needs to have validated and verified so many times under realistic conditions that it becomes automatic. Needs to know what fixation looks like from the inside — attention locked on the AI-flagged code while the complication hiding two lines up goes unread.

Literacy, in directive contexts, isn't knowing about AI. It's a practiced habit of judgment. And it emerges through repetition, not instruction.

That's what I want to talk about today.
