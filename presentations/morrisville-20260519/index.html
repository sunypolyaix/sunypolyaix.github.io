The Problem

Most AI Literacy Was Built for the Wrong Student

When higher education talks about AI literacy, it means this: a student prompts a model, gets text back, and makes decisions about what to do with that output. The evidence of their thinking is in the conversation. The product is a document.

That is the compositional case.

Almost all of the AI literacy pedagogy being developed right now — the frameworks, the rubrics, the course guidelines — was built for that case.

I want to walk you around campus, because I don't think that describes your students.

Stop One · The Dairy Farm

The Dashboard Has Already Decided What Looks Important

Robotic milking systems handle the milking. The AI monitors each animal: milk yield, conductivity, milking frequency, body weight, reproductive status. It makes feeding recommendations. It flags animals for attention.

▸ Animal #47 — yield ↓ 12% — flag: possible mastitis — recommended action: isolate + veterinary review

Your student's job is not to milk the cow. It's to read the dashboard, evaluate the flag, and decide whether to act on it.

The system has already shaped the field of attention. Your student arrives after the dashboard has decided what looks important.

The Concept

The Dashboard Is Not the Situation

That word — attention — is not accidental.

The architecture underneath many modern AI systems is literally called a transformer, built around what computer scientists call an attention mechanism: a way of weighting which signals matter most in a given context.

Humans do something similar. We constantly prioritize attention through training, instinct, experience, and judgment.

But now another attention system is entering the workflow before the human operator begins evaluating the situation.

The dashboard is not the situation. It is the model's interpretation of the situation.

Under what circumstances should your student override it? That is not a technical question. That is a literacy question.

Stop Two · The Auto Shop

The Recommendation Is the Starting Point

The first thing that happens when a vehicle comes in: someone plugs in an OBD-II scanner.

AI-assisted diagnostic platforms cross-reference trouble codes against repair records for similar vehicles, group related codes, prioritize probable causes, and present a recommended repair sequence with confidence scores.

▸ P0300 — Random Misfire — probable cause: ignition coil pack (confidence: 87%) — recommended: replace coil pack cylinders 2, 4

Your student sees the recommendation on a tablet.

The AI has already organized what looks important. Your student's attention is directed before their hands-on inspection begins.

What does it take to recognize when the recommendation is wrong? That is not a scanning skill. That is a judgment skill.

Stop Three · The Greenhouse

The System Has Often Already Acted

Irrigation systems adjust water delivery automatically. Climate control sets temperature, humidity, and CO₂ levels. Camera systems scan for disease, flag anomalies, recommend treatments.

▸ Bay 3 — botrytis risk: HIGH — sensor confidence: 94% — recommended: fungicide application within 24h

Your student receives a report. An alert. A dashboard. The system has often already acted before they see it.

What does fixation look like when the disease alert fires and attention goes to the flagged plant while the actual outbreak is two rows over?

In each of these three cases, the cognitive challenge is the same: the AI system increasingly organizes what the operator notices first.

The Evidence

Human-Factors Researchers Have Documented This

The same failure modes appear in aviation, clinical decision support, and process control when operators work alongside automated systems:

01Manual skill atrophy — procedural skills degrade from disuse
02Plan continuation bias — cognitive commitment to the AI-established course despite new cues
03Vigilance decrement — attention drifts from primary indicators
04Omission errors — failing to notice when the system misses something
05Commission errors — acting on recommendations without verification
06Confirmation bias — misinterpreting information to confirm the AI's recommendation
07Expectation-driven processing — seeing what the AI predicted, not what is actually there

What these share: the operator's attention has been pre-organized by the system, and independent judgment has been displaced by cue-following.

The Distinction

What Makes This Round of Automation Different

Many earlier automation failures were more mechanically legible. The pump breaks. The sensor malfunctions. You can see it.

This round is different in three ways:

  • The logic is opaque. The system makes a recommendation based on patterns learned from large amounts of data. You cannot always ask it why.
  • It fails by being confidently wrong. AI produces plausible-looking recommendations that happen to be incorrect — and nothing in the interface signals that.
  • It may increase cognitive demand. The AI-assisted milking system requires your student to evaluate recommendations from a system they cannot fully see into, at scale, every day.
The question is not whether students can use AI. It is whether they can maintain an independent model of the situation while AI is already organizing what they see.
The Framework

Two Different Cognitive Demands

CompositionalDirective
Human roleAuthor / editorDecision-maker / operator
AI outputText to shapeRecommendation to act on
Primary riskDelegation — accepting the first draftAutomation bias — acting without verification
Literacy requirementRevision and critical evaluationSituated judgment before action
Training environmentTranscript review and rubricScenario simulation with stakes

The AI literacy literature has developed primarily for the compositional case. The pedagogical frameworks for directive contexts are largely unbuilt.

The Pedagogy

What AI Literacy Looks Like at SUNY Morrisville

Not: teach students to use the tools. Not: teach students about automation bias.

This: design courses where students practice the moment of judgment before compliance.

  • The AI recommendation is sometimes wrong — deliberately, by design.
  • Students have to articulate why they are pausing.
  • Following the system blindly costs them something in simulation before it costs them something in the field.

Students need to learn to recognize the dashboard as model-generated rather than reality-generated — maintaining epistemic separation between what the model surfaced and what is actually happening in the barn, the shop, or the greenhouse.

That is not a new course. That is a design principle you can embed in the courses you are already teaching.

Closing

AI Literacy in Applied Fields

AI literacy in applied fields is not primarily about producing content with AI.

It is about preserving independent professional judgment while AI systems increasingly organize what appears important before humans even begin to think.

The simulation tier is the design response. You can start building it tomorrow, inside courses you are already teaching.
