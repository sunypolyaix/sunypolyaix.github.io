I'll keep this tight and practical.

**1. Defer Judgment**
AI is a judgment-free zone—it generates without gatekeeping. You can throw out half-baked ideas, contradictions, even "bad" takes, and Claude won't filter them. This lowers the mental cost of exploring weird directions. You get to *see* the idea before your internal critic kills it.

**2. Go for Quantity**
AI generates fast. Instead of one polished idea, you get five variations instantly. Quantity surfaces unlikely combinations—the statistical outlier that becomes your best insight. You can fork an idea three ways and pick the most interesting branch.

**Where AI shines for divergent thinking:**
- **Reframing**: "Show me 10 totally different angles on this problem" works better with AI than alone. Each prompt is a new lens.
- **Bridging domains**: Ask it to connect your domain to unrelated fields. AI doesn't have your mental silos, so it generates weird cross-pollinations naturally.
- **Constraint flipping**: "What if the constraint was reversed?" AI happily explores inversions you might dismiss as silly.
- **Tempo**: You can iterate *fast*—fail quickly, build on what sticks. Speed itself enables more divergence.

**The catch:** AI can also converge too fast to plausible-sounding answers. You have to actively ask for divergence ("give me the *weird* version") and stay skeptical of the first good idea.

Right—it's a mirror problem. You and AI both pattern-match to plausibility. The difference is *you know when you're doing it*, whereas with AI it feels objective because it's authoritative-sounding text.

So the meta-move becomes: **treat plausibility as a red flag, not confirmation.**

A few ways to stay skeptical:

- **Ask "why not?"** Instead of defending the first idea, actively poke holes. AI will help you here—ask it to argue *against* its own suggestion.
- **Deliberately pick the weird version.** When AI gives you option A (obvious, sensible) and option C (strange, risky), *choose C* and develop it further. You might discard it, but you'll learn something option A wouldn't have revealed.
- **Require multiple incompatible answers.** "Give me two solutions that contradict each other" forces the system (and you) past consensus-seeking.
- **Track where you both converge fastest.** Those are your blind spots—the places where pattern-matching has already won.

This connects to your RTW framework, actually. **Reading** (gathering options) + **Thinking** (holding divergence longer) + **Writing** (committing a choice) breaks the auto-convergence habit. The thinking phase is where you *resist* the first good answer.

The real skill isn't getting AI to diverge. It's training *yourself* to sit in uncertainty longer before settling.
