1. MOST AI LITERACY WAS BUILT FOR THE WRONG STUDENT
——————————————————————————————————————

When most people in higher education talk about AI literacy, they mean this:
a student prompts a model, gets text back, and makes decisions about what to
do with that output. The evidence of their thinking is in the conversation.
The product is a document, an argument, a piece of writing.

That is the compositional case. And almost all of the AI literacy pedagogy
being developed right now — the frameworks, the rubrics, the course
guidelines — was built for that case.

I want to walk you around campus for a few minutes, because I don't think
that describes your students.


——————————————————————————————————————
2. STOP ONE: THE DAIRY FARM
——————————————————————————————————————

Your students working in automated dairy operations are going to encounter a
milking parlor that looks like this.

Robotic milking systems handle the milking. The cows come and go on their own
schedule. The AI monitors each animal: milk yield, conductivity, milking
frequency, body weight, reproductive status. The system makes feeding
recommendations. It flags animals for attention. It surfaces patterns that
suggest a cow needs follow-up.

Your student's job is not to milk the cow. It's to read the dashboard,
evaluate the flag, and decide whether to act on it.

The system has already shaped the field of attention. Your student arrives
after the dashboard has decided what looks important.

And that term — attention — is not accidental.

The architecture underneath many modern AI systems is literally called a
transformer, built around what computer scientists call an attention
mechanism: a way of weighting which signals matter most in a given context.

Humans do something similar. We constantly prioritize attention through
training, instinct, experience, and judgment.

But now another attention system is entering the workflow before the human
operator begins evaluating the situation.

The dashboard is not the situation. It is the model's interpretation of
the situation.

Under what circumstances should your student override it? That's not a
technical question. That's a literacy question.


——————————————————————————————————————
3. STOP TWO: THE AUTO SHOP
——————————————————————————————————————

Your automotive technology students are going to work in shops where the
first thing that happens when a vehicle comes in is someone plugs in an
OBD-II scanner.

The scanner reads the diagnostic trouble codes. AI-assisted diagnostic
platforms cross-reference those codes against repair records for similar
vehicles, group related codes, prioritize probable causes, and present a
recommended repair sequence with confidence scores.

Your student sees the recommendation on a tablet.

The AI has already organized what looks important. Your student's attention
is directed before their hands-on inspection begins.

They might run additional tests. They might catch something the scanner
missed. But the workflow is: recommendation first, verification second —
if at all.

What does it take to recognize when the recommendation is wrong? That's
not a scanning skill. That's a judgment skill.


——————————————————————————————————————
4. STOP THREE: THE GREENHOUSE
——————————————————————————————————————

Your horticulture students are going to manage crops in environments where
AI is already making autonomous decisions.

Irrigation systems adjust water delivery based on soil moisture sensors and
weather data — without asking anyone. Climate control systems set
temperature, humidity, and CO2 levels. Camera systems scan for disease and
pest presence, flag anomalies, and recommend treatments.

The grower — your student — receives a report. An alert. A dashboard.

The system has often already acted before they see it.

What does fixation look like when the disease alert fires and attention goes
to the flagged plant while the actual outbreak is two rows over? What does
it cost when the dashboard declares the field healthy and your student
stops looking?

In each of these three cases, the cognitive challenge is the same: the AI
system increasingly organizes what the operator notices first.

5. THE COGNITIVE CHALLENGE
——————————————————————————————————————

Human-factors researchers have documented recurring failure modes when
operators work alongside automated systems. You see them in aviation, in
clinical decision support, in process control:

  1. Manual skill atrophy
     Procedural skills degrade from disuse.

  2. Plan continuation bias
     Cognitive commitment to the AI-established course despite new cues.

  3. Vigilance decrement
     Attention drifts from primary indicators during automation.

  4. Omission errors
     Failing to notice when the automation misses something.

  5. Commission errors
     Acting on automated recommendations without independent verification.

  6. Confirmation bias
     Misinterpreting ambiguous information to confirm the original plan.

  7. Expectation-driven processing
     Seeing what the AI predicted, not what is actually there.

What these have in common is not complexity. They are all failures of
attention — the operator's attention has been pre-organized by the system,
and independent judgment has been displaced by cue-following.

Aviation addressed several of these through mandatory manual flying and
scenario-based training under realistic conditions. The FAA can mandate
that because the stakes are existential — plane crashes kill people.

Your students at Morrisville are not pilots. We do not need to train them
to do everything manually as a backup.

But we do need to train them to maintain an independent model of the
situation — to recognize when their own observation of the cow, the car,
or the plant contradicts what the system is telling them, and to know
what to do with that contradiction.

That is a practiced habit of judgment. It does not emerge from instruction
about AI.

6. WHAT MAKES THIS ROUND DIFFERENT
——————————————————————————————————————

Many earlier automation failures were more mechanically legible. The pump
breaks. The sensor malfunctions. You can see it. You can trace it.

This round of AI-assisted automation is different in three ways.

First, the logic is opaque. The system makes a recommendation based on
patterns it learned from large amounts of data. You cannot always ask it
why and get a clear answer.

Second, it fails by being confidently wrong. AI can produce a
plausible-looking recommendation that happens to be incorrect for your
specific cow, your specific vehicle, your specific crop — and nothing in
the interface signals that.

Third, introducing AI into automation may actually increase the cognitive
demand on the human operator. The milking robot reduced physical labor.
The AI-assisted milking system requires your student to constantly
evaluate recommendations from a system they cannot fully see into, at
scale, every day.

The issue is not simply that AI produces recommendations. The issue is
that AI systems increasingly organize what the operator notices first —
before the operator has had a chance to form an independent assessment.


——————————————————————————————————————
7. WHAT AI LITERACY MEANS HERE
——————————————————————————————————————

Not: teach students to use the tools.

Not: teach students about automation bias.

This: design courses where students practice the moment of judgment before
compliance. Where the AI recommendation is sometimes wrong by design.
Where they have to articulate why they're pausing. Where following the
system blindly costs them something in simulation before it costs them
something in the field.

Students need to learn to recognize the dashboard as model-generated
rather than reality-generated. That is a different habit than spotting
hallucinations in a text. It is the habit of maintaining epistemic
separation between what the model surfaced and what is actually happening
in the barn, the shop, or the greenhouse.

Your dairy students should encounter scenarios where the feeding algorithm
has miscalculated and they have to catch it by reading the herd.

Your auto students should encounter vehicles where the scanner
recommendation is wrong and their hands-on inspection is the only thing
that catches it.

Your horticulture students should encounter disease alerts that are false
positives — and silent outbreaks the system missed.

That's not a new course. That's a design principle you can embed in the
courses you're already teaching.

8. CAN I GET CREDIT?
——————————————————————————————————————

AI literacy in applied fields is not primarily about producing content
with AI. It is about preserving independent professional judgment while AI
systems increasingly organize what appears important before humans even
begin to think.

The question is not whether students can use AI. It is whether they can
maintain an independent model of the situation while AI is already
organizing what they see.
