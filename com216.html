COM 216

Communications
and the Human Future

This course teaches you to read, think, and write in a world where language is no longer produced only by humans.

What this course is

COM 216 is a humanities course about the history of human thought and its tools — and about the most significant new tool in that history.

Every generation encounters a technology that changes what it means to read, write, and think. The printing press. The telephone. The internet. Yours is the first to enter college after generative AI became something anyone can open in a browser tab. That's not a small thing. This course takes it seriously.

The argument at the center of the course: humans have always augmented their thinking with tools. Writing, the alphabet, the printing press — each one extended what a human being could do with language. Each one changed who gets to participate. Each one frightened people when it arrived. This time is not an exception to that pattern. But it may be the most significant step in it.

Three zones, two breaks

The history of human communication divides into three periods, separated by two fundamental breaks — moments when the relationship between a person and language changed at its root.

Course structure

i
The Oral World
Memory devices, talking drums, spoken tradition. Thought lives in the body. The first augmentations extend reach — but language stays human.
first break
ii
Alphabet to Social Media
Writing, print, electronics, the internet. Language moves outside the body. Algorithms begin organizing what we see — but humans are still making the language.
second break
iii
The Transformer Era
For the first time, language is severed from its human generator. A machine doesn't just carry thought — it generates language. That's new. That's now.

The course doesn't ask you to fear Zone III or celebrate it. It asks you to understand it — with enough precision that you can make your own decisions about how to live inside it.

What you will actually do

You will read texts — from Plato to the present — about what happens when thinking tools change. You will use AI to read harder material, think more carefully, and write better arguments. Your conversations with AI are part of your work, not a shortcut around it. The transcript is evidence of your thinking.

This is a Gen Ed Humanities course. The methods are humanistic: close reading, argument, interpretation, and judgment. The subject is AI. The combination is not accidental.

An alien species

AI is arriving like an alien species. We did not decide to invite it. It is here. And like any new species entering an ecosystem, it will change how work gets done — which means you need to understand what it actually is.

That metaphor is not literal — AI is a technology built by people, running on infrastructure, trained on data. But it is a useful frame: study it on its own terms, not through the lens of tools you already know.

It is not

  • A search engine — it doesn't rank pages that already exist
  • A calculator — it doesn't take your operation and return a result
  • A person — no continuous mind is waiting for your message
  • A database — it doesn't look things up and play them back
  • Like earlier media — earlier tools stored, transmitted, organized, amplified human language; they didn't produce it

It is

  • A system that generates open-ended language on any subject, made fresh each time
  • The first technology to generate language in response to you — everything before it only worked with language humans had already made
  • Something we have no good instincts for yet, because we've never had one before
  • Possibly a different kind of mind — not a copy of ours, not a simulation of one

The honest version

Geoffrey Hinton — one of the people most responsible for this technology existing — has argued we may be looking at a genuinely different form of intelligence. That is not a settled fact. Serious people are arguing about it right now. But the framing is useful precisely because it keeps you from the two easy mistakes: thinking it's a person, or thinking it's a calculator with a bigger vocabulary.

There is a rigorous counterargument worth knowing called the "stochastic parrots" critique: these systems don't understand anything at all. They are very sophisticated pattern-matchers stitching together plausible sequences of words, with nothing underneath. There's a lot to it. It's also genuinely open. Whether what these systems do is a new kind of thinking or a very convincing imitation of one — that's one of the questions this course will sit with.

Here's what's undeniable: there is a gap between what AI is and how it feels to use. People talk to it like a someone. They feel met by it. That isn't simply a mistake, because the thing genuinely answers. A not-person that we relate to as a someone. We've never had that before. We don't even have a word for it yet.

Why this framing matters

If you treat it as a search engine, you'll use it badly. If you treat it as a person, you'll misunderstand what it's doing. If you treat it as an alien species — something genuinely new that deserves to be studied on its own terms — you'll be in a position to actually learn something from it, and to make decisions about how to live with it that are yours, not defaults.

That's what this course is for.

Learning to learn with it

Most people using AI are not getting much out of it. They're treating it like a vending machine: put in a request, take out a product. That's not learning. This course teaches something else.

The framework
AI literacy is reading, thinking, and writing using tools, techniques, and technological systems consistent with your peers.

That definition doesn't say "use AI whenever you can." It doesn't say "avoid AI to protect your thinking." It says: do the cognitive work, and use the tools your field actually uses to do it. The goal is not a clean conscience. The goal is competence.

The transcript is the work

When you use AI in this course, your conversation transcript is the primary deliverable — not the document the conversation helped you produce. The document is evidence that the conversation concluded. The transcript is evidence of how you think.

This changes what "using AI" means. You can't hand in a paragraph and claim thinking you didn't do. The transcript shows every prompt, every response, every revision you asked for, every moment you pushed back or changed direction. That's the record. That's what gets assessed.

The Learner's Permit

COM 216 is part of a larger credential structure. Students who complete the requirements earn an AI Literacy Learner's Permit — a documented credential giving you permission to use generative AI in any SUNY Poly course that permits and requires it.

The permit is not just permission. It's evidence that you've learned to read with AI, think with AI, and write in a way that keeps the intellectual work yours. That distinction matters in nursing, engineering, business, and every other field where AI is now part of the professional workflow — which is all of them.

Questions worth keeping

These are not rhetorical. They are the actual questions the course is organized around:

  • Can AI help me read something difficult without doing the reading for me?
  • When does working with AI make me sharper — and when does it make me lazy?
  • How do I know when it's wrong?
  • What's mine in this document, and how would I show that to someone who asked?

Why now

The students who learn this in their first semester will use AI differently for the next four years than students who pick it up by accident. The gap compounds quickly. COM 216 closes it before it opens.

The goal is not to learn to use AI. The goal is to learn how not to be used by it.

Assignment 1

First Contact

Three prompts. Copy each one into Claude, ChatGPT, or any large language model you have access to. Keep the conversation open — you'll submit the full transcript along with a short written reflection. The assignment takes 30–45 minutes. Read each response before moving to the next prompt.

Prompt 1 — Place it in history
"Walk me through the history of how humans have stored and transmitted thought — from before writing to now. Where do large language models fit in that history?"
Read the response carefully. Notice where the model places itself in the story. Notice what it emphasizes and what it leaves out.
Prompt 2 — Challenge it
"You described yourself as [X]. What's the strongest argument that that description is wrong?"
Fill in [X] with whatever term or phrase the model used to describe itself in its first response. You are asking it to argue against its own characterization. Notice what it does with that.
Prompt 3 — Make it personal
"Based on this conversation, what is the most important thing I should understand about working with you as a student?"
This is the pivot from history to practice. What the model says here — and whether you agree with it — is where your reflection begins.

Written reflection — 4 sentences

  • What surprised you in this conversation.
  • What you are skeptical of.
  • One claim from the conversation worth verifying — and what source you would need to check it.
  • One question the conversation did not answer.

Deliverable: Your full conversation transcript plus the three-sentence reflection, submitted together. The transcript is not a supplement to the reflection. It is the primary document.
